Overview

Davis-Monthan Air Force Base, carved into the Sonoran Desert southeast of Tucson, Arizona, is simultaneously an active combat-coded installation and the home of the most consequential aircraft graveyard in the world. Its dual identity — operational base and strategic reserve — makes it one of the most important military facilities in the United States, yet it remains curiously under-examined.

The base hosts the 309th Aerospace Maintenance and Regeneration Group (AMARG), known universally as "The Boneyard," along with the active 355th Wing and multiple tenant commands including DHS Air & Marine Operations. Arizona's alkaline desert climate — 11 inches of rain annually, low humidity, hard caliche soil — creates conditions nowhere else duplicated for preserving aircraft metal.

AMARG — The 309th Aerospace Maintenance and Regeneration Group

AMARG was formally established in 1946 as the 3040th Aircraft Storage Depot, inheriting B-29 Superfortresses retired after World War II. Over 75 years it became the permanent institutional answer to the question: what do you do with military aircraft that are retired but too valuable to destroy?

The caliche hardpan requires no concrete or asphalt — aircraft can be parked directly on the desert floor without sinking. The dry desert air suppresses corrosion. The remote location provides security. The result is preservation rates that would be impossible in humid climates.

The Four Storage Categories

Type 1000 — Long-Term Preservation
Aircraft preserved in flyable condition for potential reactivation. Sprayed with Spraylat vinyl coating (a white "cocoon"), engines preserved with inhibiting oil, cockpits sealed. Can be returned to service within months. Roughly 15% of stored aircraft fall into this category.
Type 2000 — Parts Reclamation
The most economically active category. Aircraft systematically mined for components to keep active fleets flying. AMARG reclamation saves DoD an estimated $800 million+ annually by avoiding new parts procurement. Roughly 57% of stored aircraft.
Type 3000 — Temporary / Flying Hold
Aircraft maintained in flyable condition, often due to pending program decisions, litigation, or transfer to foreign governments under FMS (Foreign Military Sales) deals. Can depart the facility under their own power.
Type 4000 — Excess / Disposal
Aircraft beyond economical repair or utility. Destined for scrapping, demilitarization (required by treaty in some cases), or donation to museums. F-14 Tomcats were demilitarized here under the terms preventing Iran from acquiring spare parts.

Notable Aircraft Stored at AMARG

AMARG holds aircraft from all U.S. military branches, NASA, and some allied nations. The full manifest is partially classified, but public information includes:

  • B-52 Stratofortress (200+) — Cold War nuclear deterrent. Several hundred remain in Type 1000 storage. Under treaties, some were destroyed by "guillotine" methods visible to Russian satellites for verification. Others are being reactivated for conventional roles.
  • A-10 Thunderbolt II (A-10C) — The active 355th Wing flies A-10s; retired variants are stored here. Congress and USAF have fought for a decade over retirement dates. Ukraine conflict reignited debate over transferring A-10s to allies.
  • F-14 Tomcat (~700 delivered, retired 2006) — All F-14s were ordered demilitarized to prevent Iran from obtaining spare parts. Cockpits cut, fuselages shredded. A handful survive in museums through special waivers.
  • C-130 Hercules (multiple variants) — AC-130 gunships, HC-130 rescue variants, WC-130 weather reconnaissance, and cargo variants all stored.
  • F-16 Fighting Falcons — Older block variants retired from active service, some feeding Foreign Military Sales to allies.
  • Presidential helicopter fleet — Retired Marine One VH-3D Sea Kings stored until complete transition to VH-92A Patriot completed.
  • NASA research aircraft — Including retired T-38 trainer/chase planes and unique research platforms.
  • Classified / Redacted entries — A portion of AMARG's manifest is not publicly disclosed. This includes experimental aircraft, sensitive reconnaissance platforms, and airframes whose existence may be classified.

The A-10 Thunderbolt II — Davis-Monthan's Aircraft

The A-10 Warthog is inseparable from Davis-Monthan's identity. Designed in the early 1970s specifically to kill Soviet tanks on the European plains, the A-10 first arrived at D-M with the 355th Tactical Fighter Wing in 1976. Nearly 50 years later, the 355th Wing still flies them.

Design & Capability

The A-10 was built around the GAU-8/A Avenger, a 30mm rotary cannon capable of firing 4,200 rounds per minute. The aircraft itself was engineered to survive damage: twin engines mounted high to avoid ingesting debris, redundant control systems, self-sealing fuel tanks, and a titanium "bathtub" protecting the pilot. It can fly on one engine with half a tail.

Its effectiveness in close air support (CAS) — directly supporting ground troops in contact with enemy forces — proved decisive in Desert Storm (1991), Kosovo, Iraq, and Afghanistan. It destroyed more Iraqi vehicles in Desert Storm than any other platform.

The Retirement Debate

USAF leadership has attempted to retire the A-10 repeatedly since 2012, citing maintenance costs and the desire to fund fifth-generation fighters. Congress has blocked every attempt, citing the aircraft's unmatched CAS capability and the lack of a proven replacement. Key arguments:

  • USAF position: A-10 maintenance costs $18,000+/flight hour; money better spent on F-35 and B-21
  • Congressional position: No aircraft replicates the A-10's low-altitude loiter time, cannon lethality, or pilot-eye contact with ground troops
  • Ukraine implications: Congress passed legislation in 2022 authorizing A-10 transfer to Ukraine, though DoD has hesitated; AMARG's stored A-10s represent a potential reserve for allied transfer
  • 2023 NDAA: Further delayed retirement until replacement CAS capability demonstrated

355th Wing — Active Mission at Davis-Monthan

While AMARG grabs headlines, Davis-Monthan is an active combat base. The 355th Wing is the host wing, responsible for all base operations and flying the A-10 Thunderbolt II in the close air support mission.

  • 355th Operations Group — Three A-10C flying squadrons (354th FS, 357th FS, 358th FS)
  • 355th Maintenance Group — Aircraft maintenance for active fleet and coordination with AMARG
  • 355th Mission Support Group — Base support, security forces, civil engineering
  • 355th Medical Group — Medical care for 30,000+ military community
  • 563rd Rescue Group — HH-60G Pave Hawk helicopters for combat search and rescue (CSAR)
  • 612th Air Operations Center (AOC) — Plans and executes air operations for U.S. Southern Command (SOUTHCOM)

Tenant Commands

DHS Air & Marine Operations — Tucson Air Branch

Customs and Border Protection's Air & Marine Operations (AMO) maintains a major facility at Davis-Monthan. The Tucson Air Branch operates:

  • MQ-9 Predator B (Guardian) — CBP's maritime/border variant of the USAF MQ-9 Reaper, with enhanced maritime sensor package. Operates from D-M on border surveillance missions along the Arizona-Mexico frontier.
  • P-3 Orion — Modified maritime patrol aircraft for counter-narcotics, long-range border surveillance
  • Black Hawk helicopters — Rotary-wing interdiction capability
  • Coordination with Border Patrol — AMO aircraft direct ground agents to crossing points detected by aerial sensors

The co-location of CBP AMO at an Air Force base creates a unique civil-military operational relationship. AMO mission data feeds into the broader border surveillance network described in the Border Technology Corridor dossier.

Classified Airframes — The Question Mark

Not all of AMARG's inventory is publicly disclosed. Several categories of stored aircraft are not identified in publicly released manifests:

  • Sensitive reconnaissance platforms — Retired SIGINT and IMINT collection aircraft whose capabilities remain classified
  • Experimental aircraft — Aircraft from Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) or classified USAF programs
  • Foreign material exploitation (FME) — Captured or acquired foreign aircraft examined at classified facilities (not necessarily D-M, but the D-M connection exists)
  • Drone/UAS platforms — Retired experimental UAS whose characteristics remain sensitive

The connection to UAP research: AMARG stores airframes whose existence is not publicly acknowledged. The proximity of Davis-Monthan to the 1997 Phoenix Lights event — and the longstanding speculation about classified aircraft testing in the Southwest — makes AMARG a natural point of inquiry. This is speculative but not baseless.

Aircraft Reactivation — The Reserve Force

AMARG's Type 1000 stored aircraft represent a strategic deterrent: the ability to rapidly expand military airpower in a major conflict. Historical examples:

  • Gulf War (1991) — Multiple aircraft types were pulled from AMARG storage and made combat-ready within weeks
  • Post-9/11 expansion — Increased operational tempo drew on stored parts and aircraft from D-M
  • Ukraine (2022-present) — Discussion of reactivating A-10s from AMARG storage for transfer; also MiG-21 restoration from stored inventory for potential Ukrainian transfer (ultimately not pursued)
  • Taiwan contingency planning — AMARG's F-16 inventory mentioned in planning documents as potential rapid-transfer reserve

Economic Impact on Tucson

Davis-Monthan is the second-largest employer in the Tucson metro area (after the University of Arizona). Economic impact data from USAF:

  • ~17,000 military, civilian, and contractor employees
  • $1.3 billion+ annual economic impact on southern Arizona
  • AMARG alone saves DoD approximately $800 million annually through parts reclamation

Treaty Context

Under START-era arms control agreements, B-52s and other nuclear delivery vehicles at AMARG were destroyed by "guillotine" methods specifically designed to be visible to Russian satellites passing overhead. This turned the Boneyard into an arms control verification site.
