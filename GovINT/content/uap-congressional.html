UAP Congressional Disclosure
The Paper Trail from AATIP to AARO — 2017 to Present
OFFICIAL RECORD SWORN TESTIMONY DOSSIER

Overview

The modern era of UAP (Unidentified Aerial Phenomena) Congressional disclosure began in earnest in 2017 with the New York Times exposé revealing the existence of the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), a classified Pentagon study that ran from 2007 to 2012 with approximately $22 million in funding, administered through a Senate earmark championed by Senator Harry Reid (D-NV). AATIP was run out of the Defense Intelligence Agency and led by counterintelligence officer Luis Elizondo. The program produced technical reports on UAP materials, physics, and biology that remain partially classified.

In August 2020, the Department of Defense officially established the Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force (UAPTF) within the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security. The UAPTF was charged with detecting, analyzing, and cataloguing UAP incidents that could threaten national security, and its mandate directly acknowledged that UAP were a legitimate intelligence and safety concern rather than a fringe phenomenon.

Congress institutionalized the effort further in July 2022 when the FY2022 National Defense Authorization Act established the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) under the Office of the Secretary of Defense. AARO assumed the UAPTF mission and expanded it across all domains — air, sea, space, and subsurface — with a mandate to report to Congress annually and to maintain a secure mechanism for military and intelligence personnel to report UAP encounters without fear of reprisal.

The legislative arc from AATIP to AARO represents a fundamental shift: UAP are no longer a taboo subject in the halls of Congress. They are now a line item, an office, a statutory requirement, and the subject of sworn testimony before the full House of Representatives.

Key Legislative Milestones

The following milestones trace the legislative codification of UAP oversight from 2020 onward:

  • FY2021 Intelligence Authorization Act (IAA) — The Senate Intelligence Committee, chaired by Sen. Marco Rubio, inserted a provision directing the DNI and SecDef to produce a comprehensive UAP report within 180 days. This resulted in the June 2021 ODNI Preliminary Assessment.
  • May 17, 2022 — First Open Congressional Hearing in Over 50 Years — The House Intelligence Committee Subcommittee on Counterterrorism, Counterintelligence, and Counterproliferation held the first open hearing on UAP since the Air Force's Project Blue Book era. Deputy SecDef Ronald Moultrie and UAPTF Director Scott Bray testified. The UAP database at that point contained 400+ reports.
  • FY2022 NDAA — AARO Established — Section 1683 of the FY2022 National Defense Authorization Act formally created the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, with reporting requirements, a historical UAP records review mandate, and protection provisions for UAP reporters.
  • 2023 UAP Disclosure Act (S.2226) — Senators Chuck Schumer (D-NY) and Mike Rounds (R-SD) introduced the UAP Disclosure Act of 2023, modeled on the JFK Assassination Records Collection Act. The bill would have established a presidentially appointed review board with subpoena power to declassify UAP records held across all government agencies and private contractors. The provision was inserted into the FY2024 NDAA but significantly weakened in conference, removing the review board's authority over private entities.
  • FY2024 NDAA Provisions — The enacted FY2024 NDAA included requirements for AARO to submit a historical UAP records report (Vol. 1 published February 2024), a classified annex requirement, and provisions directing the Executive Branch to preserve all UAP records. Sen. Mark Kelly (D-AZ) voted in the Armed Services Committee for expanded disclosure requirements.

The Grusch Testimony (July 26, 2023)

On July 26, 2023, the House Committee on Oversight and Accountability held a hearing titled "Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena: Implications for National Security, Public Safety, and Government Transparency." Three witnesses testified under oath, making the proceeding a matter of Congressional record:

David Grusch — A former intelligence officer with the National Reconnaissance Office (NRO) and the NGA who served as the UAP integration and outreach representative for the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency until 2023. Grusch testified that, in the course of his official duties, he received information from senior officials about classified UAP programs involving non-human origin craft. Under oath, he stated:

"I was informed in the course of my official duties of a multi-decade UAP crash retrieval and reverse-engineering program to which I was denied access."

Grusch further testified that he had provided classified testimony to Congressional intelligence committees, had filed a whistleblower complaint through the Inspector General of the Intelligence Community (ICIG), and that he had knowledge of individuals who had been harmed or threatened in connection with UAP programs. He declined to name specific programs in open session but indicated a classified session had been arranged.

Commander David Fravor (ret.) — The Navy pilot who led the 2004 encounter with the "Tic Tac" UAP off the coast of San Diego during exercise operations from USS Nimitz. Fravor described an object approximately 40 feet in length, with no visible propulsion, no wings, no rotors, and no exhaust. The object demonstrated instantaneous acceleration and disappearance. Fravor testified: "I can tell you that what I saw is real. I would like to say that I don't think we should be this technologically inferior to whatever this is."

Ryan Graves — A former Navy F/A-18 pilot and founder of Americans for Safe Aerospace. Graves testified to routine encounters with UAP in restricted military training airspace off the Atlantic coast, beginning around 2014 and continuing for years. He stated that pilots routinely observed UAP in training areas but that a stigma around reporting deterred formal documentation. Graves indicated that the pattern of encounters near sensitive military airspace was not accidental.

AARO Historical Record Report, Volume 1 (February 2024)
AARO • February 2024 • Unclassified
AARO's congressionally mandated historical review of U.S. government UAP programs dating to the 1940s. Reviews Project Sign, Grudge, Blue Book, and post-Blue Book classified programs. Finds no verifiable evidence of non-human intelligence programs. Critics including Grusch dispute the report's completeness, citing compartmentalization that would prevent AARO from accessing all relevant records.

NASA UAP Independent Study Team Final Report (2023)
NASA • September 2023 • Public
Commissioned by NASA Administrator Bill Nelson. A 16-member independent team assessed NASA's potential role in UAP investigation. Stops short of attributing UAP to non-human origin but does not foreclose the possibility. Notes that stigma impedes scientific study.

Arizona Connection

Arizona occupies a unique position in the UAP Congressional disclosure landscape, both geographically and politically.

Military Installations: Arizona hosts several of the nation's most significant military facilities within or adjacent to the corridor most often cited in UAP Congressional testimony:

  • Davis-Monthan AFB (Tucson) — Home to the 355th Wing and the Aerospace Maintenance and Regeneration Group (AMARG), the world's largest aircraft storage facility. Also houses A-10 and EC-130 units. UAP researchers and FOIA requestors have repeatedly queried AMARG records related to unidentified recovered aerospace materials.
  • Luke AFB (Glendale/West Phoenix metro) — Primary F-35 pilot training base for the nation. Airspace management over western Arizona is coordinated through Luke. The 1997 Phoenix Lights event occurred in airspace adjacent to Luke's training zones, and the base denied any unusual activity that night.
  • Fort Huachuca (Sierra Vista) — U.S. Army Intelligence Center of Excellence. Home to the Army's primary intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) training programs and significant SIGINT collection infrastructure. Fort Huachuca's ISR capabilities would theoretically encompass some of the most sensitive electronic monitoring of Arizona airspace.
  • Barry Goldwater Range — Spans Maricopa and Yuma counties. One of the largest military training ranges in the continental U.S. The range was cited in both the 1997 Phoenix Lights case (flare drops) and is regularly used for classified weapons testing. Range airspace management includes restricted zones where UAP behavior consistent with Congressional testimony has been reported.

Grusch's Southwestern Claims: While Grusch declined to specify locations in open session, his Congressional testimony and subsequent media interviews have alluded to alleged recovery and storage sites in the American Southwest. Arizona, Nevada, and New Mexico form a geographic cluster repeatedly cited in historical UAP claims (Roswell 1947, Kingman 1953, and multiple later incidents). The proximity of classified facilities to civilian population centers in Arizona creates a unique overlap.

Senator Mark Kelly: Arizona's senior U.S. Senator Mark Kelly (D-AZ), a former NASA astronaut and Navy combat pilot with F-14/F-18 background, serves on the Senate Armed Services Committee. Kelly has publicly supported greater UAP transparency, stating that pilots deserve a stigma-free reporting environment. Kelly's aviation and astronaut background gives his UAP positions particular credibility in military aviation circles.

What's Still Classified

The public record makes clear that significant UAP-related information remains withheld from public and even most Congressional access. Based on testimony, FOIA rejections, and official statements, the following categories are believed to remain classified at SAP (Special Access Program) or higher levels:

  • JWICS-level sensor data — Raw signals intelligence, radar, and electro-optical data collected on UAP encounters through classified collection systems. The unclassified ODNI reports explicitly acknowledge that the most significant data cannot be shared publicly due to methods and sources protection.
  • SAP compartment inventories — The specific names, structures, and budgets of any Special Access Programs examining UAP. The FY2024 NDAA required a classified annex to AARO's report but the existence and contents of such SAPs remains unconfirmed in open sources.
  • Recovered material claims — Grusch's testimony specifically addressed alleged physical recovered materials from UAP incidents. No official confirmation of recovered non-human materials has been provided. AARO's Vol. 1 report found no evidence of such programs in records it was able to review, but acknowledged its access was not unlimited.
  • Biological evidence claims — Grusch testified in classified session about claims related to biological material associated with UAP incidents. No public confirmation exists. Multiple Congressional members who have received classified briefings have made statements suggesting the classified record contains more than the public record reflects.
  • Private contractor holdings — The failure to include private contractor subpoena authority in the final FY2024 NDAA means that any UAP-related materials held by defense contractors (rather than government agencies) remain outside the government's current transparency framework.
