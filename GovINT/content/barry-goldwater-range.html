Overview

The Barry M. Goldwater Range (BMGR) is the largest combined air and ground training range in the United States — and one of the largest in the world. Stretching across southwestern Arizona from the outskirts of Phoenix to the Mexican border, it occupies 2.7 million acres of Sonoran Desert that has been restricted from public access for over 80 years. It is named for Senator Barry Goldwater, who served on the Senate Armed Services Committee and was a brigadier general in the Air Force Reserve.

The BMGR is divided into two separate areas with different managing agencies:

  • BMGR East: Administered by Luke Air Force Base (56th Fighter Wing). Located south of the Gila River, north of Ajo. This is where most combat aircraft training occurs.
  • BMGR West: Administered by Marine Corps Air Station Yuma (3rd Marine Aircraft Wing). Located along the Arizona-California border and into the Yuma area.

Together they represent airspace where every form of live-fire air-to-ground combat training, air-to-air combat maneuvering, electronic warfare, and next-generation weapons testing occurs — away from public observation.

What Happens on the Range

Live-Fire Bombing

The BMGR contains multiple bombing ranges with established impact areas where live ordnance is dropped. These range from simple Mk-82 500-pound bombs to precision munitions, cruise missiles, and classified next-generation weapons under test. Key bombing areas include:

  • Gila Bend Air Force Auxiliary Field (GBAF): The main support installation for the East range. Located near Gila Bend, AZ. Provides fuel, maintenance, and emergency services for aircraft using the range.
  • Stovepipe bombing range: One of several established impact areas where vehicles, mock structures, and pre-positioned targets are engaged.
  • Electronic Combat Range: Where aircraft practice suppression of enemy air defenses (SEAD) — attacking simulated radar, SAM sites, and communications nodes using electronic warfare and anti-radiation missiles.

Air-to-Air Combat Training

The Military Operations Areas (MOAs) above the BMGR are where fighter pilots practice aerial combat maneuvering (ACM). Several designated MOAs are active over southwestern Arizona:

  • Goldwater MOA and associated warning areas — Where F-35As from Luke AFB, F-35Bs from MCAS Yuma, and visiting aircraft practice BFM (Basic Fighter Maneuver), dissimilar air combat training (DACT), and large force exercises
  • Warning Areas W-289, W-291, W-299 — Offshore and overland warning areas bordering the range where supersonic flight and weapons testing occur
  • Pilots from Nellis AFB (Weapons School, Red Flag exercises), Eglin AFB, and visiting allied air forces regularly use the range

Ground Combat Training

The BMGR includes ground maneuver areas where military units conduct large-scale training exercises:

  • Combined arms exercises involving armor, infantry, artillery, and close air support
  • Special operations training — the remote terrain mimics multiple operational environments
  • Marine Corps ground forces from MCAS Yuma and Camp Pendleton conduct desert warfare training
  • Army units rotate through for desert operations certification

Electronic Warfare Testing

The BMGR is a primary testing site for electronic warfare systems — both airborne EW pods carried by aircraft and ground-based radar/jamming systems. The range's isolation from commercial airspace and the low electromagnetic background noise of the remote desert make it ideal for EW testing. Classified EW systems are evaluated here before fielding.

F-35 Training — Luke AFB's Primary Range

Luke AFB is the largest F-35A training base in the world, and the Goldwater Range is its primary training area. The 56th Fighter Wing trains U.S. Air Force, Air National Guard, Air Force Reserve, and foreign military students in the F-35A. Nations that train at Luke include Australia, Denmark, Israel, Italy, Netherlands, Norway, Poland, and South Korea — all of whom use the BMGR to train their future F-35 pilots in live-fire and combat maneuvering scenarios.

The volume of activity is staggering: on a peak training day, 100+ sorties may transit the BMGR, including weapons deliveries, radar employment, electronic warfare, and air-to-air combat. The range handles more combat air training volume than almost any other facility in the world.

The Tohono O'odham Complication

The BMGR West, administered by MCAS Yuma, shares its southern boundary with the Tohono O'odham Nation's reservation. The O'odham's traditional territory was already divided by the international boundary with Mexico; the military range further restricts their movement and access in the area.

Historical context: the Papago Indian Reservation (the Tohono O'odham's previous federal designation) was in existence when the military range was established in 1941. The range boundaries were drawn over and around the reservation, creating a complex jurisdictional situation. O'odham community members who need to cross restricted areas to access traditional use sites, cultural resources, or the border crossing into Mexico must navigate range safety restrictions and military authorities.

Wildlife & Environmental Conflicts

The BMGR is also the Cabeza Prieta National Wildlife Refuge (overlap in the West sector) and the Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument borders the range. The Sonoran pronghorn (Antilocapra americana sonoriensis), one of the most endangered mammals in North America, has its only U.S. habitat partly within and adjacent to the range.

The Air Force and USFWS have an ongoing management agreement attempting to balance combat training requirements with pronghorn recovery. Sonoran pronghorn cannot coexist with high-frequency low-altitude jet noise during birthing season. Training schedules have been modified to reduce impact during critical periods — but the tension between military requirements and endangered species law is permanent and legally complex.

Unexploded Ordnance (UXO)

After 80+ years of live-fire training, the BMGR contains significant unexploded ordnance in its impact areas. The Air Force maintains records of ordnance drops and conducts periodic range clearance, but the total quantity of UXO present is unknown — some impact areas have never been systematically cleared. This creates:

  • Permanent public exclusion from most of the range's interior
  • Liability issues for any future change in land use
  • Environmental contamination concerns (lead, chemical residues from explosives)
  • Humanitarian concerns: migrants crossing through the BMGR have encountered UXO

The Border Intersection

The BMGR's southern boundary runs along the U.S.-Mexico border. This creates an unusual situation: the most heavily surveilled, restricted military zone in the continental U.S. shares its southern boundary with one of the most active smuggling and migration corridors in the hemisphere. CBP Border Patrol and Air & Marine Operations conduct enforcement operations in areas adjacent to, and sometimes overlapping with, military range boundaries. Military operations and border enforcement share airspace and occasionally coordinate, though the agencies operate under separate authorities.

The 2006-2008 "Operation Jump Start" National Guard border support mission involved Guard units supporting CBP in areas adjacent to the BMGR. Guard aviation assets from Davis-Monthan trained in BMGR corridors and then supported border surveillance missions.

What Isn't Public

The BMGR's isolation and restricted access make it a logical location for programs that require large-scale physical testing away from observation. Things not publicly confirmed that have been speculated or reported:

  • Next-generation aircraft testing: The BMGR's restricted airspace and proximity to Edwards AFB (California) and other test facilities makes it a natural extension of the classified flight test corridor. Low-observable aircraft (stealth) require large, sparsely observed airspace for testing.
  • Directed energy weapons: High-power microwave and laser weapons require remote test environments. The BMGR's isolation is ideal.
  • Autonomous systems: Next-generation autonomous vehicles and UAS require unrestricted maneuvering space for testing AI decision-making in contested environments.
  • Electronic warfare classified programs: Testing of classified SIGINT/ELINT collection capabilities against live military emitters.

None of these are confirmed in public documents. The point is not assertion but understanding: what we know publicly about BMGR activity represents a fraction of what actually occurs.
