Overview

Arizona occupies a unique position in American cybersecurity: it is simultaneously a training ground, a target, and a node of critical military infrastructure. The state hosts the Army's global network command, the largest nuclear plant in the country, contested election infrastructure, and a rapidly growing technology sector. Understanding Arizona's cyber landscape means understanding the intersection of physical infrastructure and digital vulnerability.

This is not abstract risk. In 2021, a hacker broke into a water treatment facility in Oldsmar, Florida, and attempted to increase sodium hydroxide to dangerous levels. The Oldsmar attack used the same type of remote access software found in dozens of Arizona water utility systems. The threat is real, present, and in many cases, inadequately defended.

Fort Huachuca & NETCOM — The Military Cyber Backbone

What NETCOM Actually Does

The U.S. Army Network Enterprise Technology Command (NETCOM), headquartered at Fort Huachuca, operates the Army Enterprise Network (AEN) — the global digital infrastructure connecting every Army installation, headquarters, and deployed unit worldwide. NETCOM's scale is difficult to comprehend:

  • 350,000+ network endpoints across all Army installations, from Fort Wainwright, Alaska to Grafenwöhr, Germany
  • 300+ installations managed through four Regional Cyber Centers (RCCs): CONUS, Europe, Pacific, and Southwest Asia
  • 24/7 defensive cyber operations: NETCOM's soldiers and contractors monitor Army networks around the clock, managing intrusion detection, incident response, and threat hunting across the global enterprise
  • LANDWARNET: The overarching Army network architecture that NETCOM manages — a classified and unclassified network stack supporting tactical, operational, and strategic Army communications

Army Cyber Command Coordination

NETCOM works in close coordination with U.S. Army Cyber Command (ARCYBER), headquartered at Fort Gordon, Georgia. While NETCOM defends the Army's network infrastructure, ARCYBER conducts offensive and defensive cyber operations as a component of U.S. Cyber Command (USCYBERCOM). The intelligence professionals trained at Fort Huachuca's Army Intelligence Center of Excellence (USAICoE) feed directly into both organizations.

The Fort Huachuca–Fort Gordon pipeline represents one of the most significant concentrations of military cyber talent in the country. The training pipeline runs from Sierra Vista to Augusta, producing the analysts, network defenders, and intelligence professionals who protect and exploit digital networks globally.

Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station

The Facility

Located 55 miles west of Phoenix near Tonopah, Arizona, Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station is the largest nuclear power plant in the United States by electricity generation — larger than any plant in the nation. Operated by Arizona Public Service (APS), Palo Verde generates approximately 3,937 megawatts of electricity from its three pressurized water reactors, supplying power to parts of Arizona, California, New Mexico, and Texas.

Critically: Palo Verde is the only nuclear plant in the world not located near a major body of water. Instead of river or ocean cooling, it uses treated municipal wastewater from the Phoenix metro area — 20 billion gallons of water per year.

NRC Cybersecurity Requirements — 10 CFR Part 73.54

Following the recognition that nuclear facilities are high-value cyber targets, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) implemented 10 CFR Part 73.54, "Protection of Digital Computer and Communication Systems and Networks," which became effective June 2009. Key requirements applied to Palo Verde:

  • Defense-in-depth: Multiple independent layers of cybersecurity controls — not a single perimeter. Attack must penetrate multiple barriers before reaching safety systems.
  • Critical Digital Asset (CDA) identification: Every digital system that could affect safety, security, or emergency preparedness is catalogued and protected
  • Air gap requirements: Safety-critical systems (reactor control, emergency core cooling, containment) must be isolated from networks that connect to the internet or corporate networks
  • Insider threat program: Background screening, access controls, and behavioral monitoring for personnel with access to protected digital systems
  • Cyber drills: Regular tabletop and functional exercises testing the facility's ability to detect, respond to, and recover from a cyber intrusion
  • NRC inspections: Periodic NRC cybersecurity inspections with findings reported publicly (some details redacted)

Despite these protections, Palo Verde remains a high-value target. Nation-state actors with the capability to attack nuclear infrastructure include Russia (the SolarWinds campaign compromised some energy sector networks) and China (multiple campaigns against U.S. critical infrastructure documented in CISA advisories).

NERC CIP Standards — The Electric Grid

Arizona's electric utilities — primarily Arizona Public Service (APS) and Salt River Project (SRP) — must comply with NERC Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) standards, developed by the North American Electric Reliability Corporation. These are mandatory cybersecurity requirements for bulk electric system operators:

  • CIP-002: BES Cyber System Categorization — identifying which systems are critical and how critical
  • CIP-003/004: Security management controls and personnel training requirements
  • CIP-005: Electronic security perimeters — defining and defending network boundaries around critical systems
  • CIP-006/007: Physical security of BES cyber systems; systems security management (patching, ports, vulnerability management)
  • CIP-008/009: Incident reporting and response; recovery plans
  • CIP-010: Configuration change management and vulnerability assessments
  • CIP-013: Supply chain risk management — enacted after concerns about compromised hardware from foreign suppliers entering utility infrastructure

NERC CIP violations carry fines up to $1 million per violation per day. Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC) is the regional entity overseeing compliance in Arizona.

Salt River Project (SRP) — Phoenix Metro Grid

SRP is a multi-purpose water and power agency serving 1 million+ electricity customers in the Phoenix metro. Its SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) systems manage power generation from multiple sources: Palo Verde (nuclear), natural gas peakers, hydroelectric on the Salt River, and a growing renewable portfolio. SRP's SCADA infrastructure, like all large utility control systems, is a persistent target for reconnaissance by sophisticated adversaries. The integration of legacy control systems with modern IT networks creates attack surface — the same challenge faced by every utility in the country.

Water Infrastructure SCADA — A Critical Vulnerability

Arizona's water delivery systems are controlled by industrial control systems (ICS) and SCADA networks. The Central Arizona Project's 336-mile canal, 14 pumping plants, and associated infrastructure rely on automated control systems that can be — and historically have been — vulnerable to cyberattack.

The Oldsmar Comparison

In February 2021, a hacker using TeamViewer remote access software connected to the Oldsmar, Florida water treatment plant's SCADA system and attempted to increase sodium hydroxide (lye) levels by 11,000% — potentially poisoning the water supply for 15,000 residents. A plant operator noticed the cursor moving on his screen and reversed the change. The hack used a remote access tool also found in dozens of smaller Arizona water systems.

CISA and the EPA have since issued joint advisories warning water utilities about legacy software, default credentials, and internet-exposed HMIs (Human Machine Interfaces). Many small Arizona municipalities — serving irrigation districts, rural communities, and tribal water systems — have not fully implemented these recommendations due to resource constraints.

CISA Advisories Relevant to Arizona Infrastructure

  • AA21-042A: Compromise of U.S. Water Treatment Facility — post-Oldsmar advisory on ICS security for water utilities
  • AA22-057A: Destructive Malware Targeting Ukrainian Organizations — context for similar techniques applicable to U.S. critical infrastructure
  • AA23-061A: #StopRansomware — Royal Ransomware — specifically notes targeting of U.S. critical infrastructure including utilities
  • ICS-CERT advisories: Multiple advisories specifically targeting Schneider Electric and Siemens industrial control systems widely deployed in Arizona utilities

Election Security — The 2020 Maricopa Audit

Arizona became the national epicenter of election security controversy following the 2020 presidential election. Maricopa County, home to 4.4 million residents and approximately 60% of Arizona's votes, became the focus of what became known as the "Cyber Ninjas audit" — a partisan review of the 2020 election results commissioned by the Arizona State Senate.

The Cyber Ninjas Audit (2021)

  • The Arizona State Senate contracted Cyber Ninjas, a previously obscure cybersecurity firm, to audit 2.1 million Maricopa County ballots in April-September 2021
  • The audit was widely criticized by election security experts for methodology, chain of custody concerns, and the use of personnel without election administration experience
  • Cyber Ninjas had no established track record in election auditing. The firm's CEO had publicly promoted election fraud theories.
  • The final report, released September 2021, confirmed Biden won Maricopa County but raised unsubstantiated allegations about ballot irregularities
  • CISA's election security team, which works with state election officials on cybersecurity guidance, does not typically engage with post-election partisan audits of this nature
  • Cyber Ninjas subsequently dissolved in January 2022 after failing to pay employees

CISA Election Security Guidance

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) provides election security resources to all 50 states. Relevant to Arizona:

  • Election Infrastructure ISAC (EI-ISAC): Funded by CISA, provides threat intelligence, vulnerability scanning, and incident response support to state and county election officials. Arizona Secretary of State's office participates.
  • #Protect2024 initiative: CISA's election security program offering free services to election officials including network scanning, tabletop exercises, and physical security assessments
  • Voting system standards: Arizona uses voting systems certified under Voluntary Voting System Guidelines (VVSG). Maricopa County uses Dominion Voting Systems equipment, which has been subject to extensive security research and review.
  • Paper ballot requirement: Arizona uses paper ballots for all voting — a key security feature that allows for physical audits independent of electronic systems

Ransomware in Arizona — Documented Incidents

Arizona municipalities and institutions have been targeted by ransomware attacks, consistent with national trends affecting local governments:

  • Town of Florence, AZ (2019): Ransomware attack disrupted town computer systems. Paid ransom to recover operations.
  • Arizona school districts (multiple): Several Arizona K-12 school districts have experienced ransomware attacks, consistent with nationwide targeting of education sector institutions
  • Maricopa County (2020): County election systems were subject to attempted intrusion attempts documented in a CISA joint report — no successful compromise confirmed
  • Healthcare sector: Arizona hospitals and healthcare networks have been extensively targeted by ransomware groups including REvil and BlackCat/ALPHV; Banner Health (Phoenix) experienced a major breach in 2016 affecting 3.7 million patients

Arizona's Cyber Workforce Pipeline

Arizona has a significant and growing cybersecurity workforce driven by military training (Fort Huachuca), academic programs, and corporate investment:

  • Arizona State University (ASU): NSA-designated National Center of Academic Excellence in Cyber Defense Research (CAE-R). The Global Security Initiative at ASU conducts policy-relevant cyber research.
  • University of Arizona (UA): CAE-designated cybersecurity programs. Strong computer science and engineering pipeline feeding Intel, Raytheon, and defense contractors in Tucson.
  • Estrella Mountain / Mesa Community Colleges: Community college CTE programs producing entry-level cybersecurity analysts for the Phoenix market
  • Arizona Cyber Threat Response Alliance (ACTRA): Public-private partnership for cybersecurity information sharing among Arizona businesses and government agencies
  • Corporate concentration: Intel, Microchip Technology, ON Semiconductor, TSMC (under construction in Phoenix), Go Daddy, GoDaddy, PayPal — major tech and finance employers creating demand for cybersecurity professionals
