Overview — Nations Within a State

Arizona contains the largest Native American land area of any state in the contiguous United States. Approximately 28% of Arizona's total land area — nearly 20 million acres — is held in federal trust for tribal nations. This is not historical artifact; it is a living governmental structure with direct implications for water, border security, environmental regulation, gaming revenue, and federal appropriations.

Tribal governments in Arizona are sovereign entities with a government-to-government relationship with the United States, not merely interest groups or ethnic minorities within the state system. Their water rights, established by the Supreme Court in 1963 as "senior" to state law, may be the most consequential legal status in Arizona's future as the Colorado River crisis deepens.

The Legal Foundation — Federal Trust and Sovereignty

The Federal Trust Relationship

The United States holds land "in trust" for tribal nations — meaning the federal government holds legal title but tribal nations have the right to use and benefit from the land, with the federal government obligated to protect those interests. This fiduciary responsibility is:

  • Enforceable in federal court
  • Funded through Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) appropriations
  • The basis for federal programs including Indian Health Service, Bureau of Indian Education, and tribal housing programs
  • A source of ongoing litigation — tribes sue the federal government regularly for failures of trust management

The Winters Doctrine — Senior Water Rights

In Winters v. United States (1908), the Supreme Court established that when Congress created Indian reservations, it implicitly reserved sufficient water to make the reservations viable — and that these rights dated to the establishment of the reservation, making them senior to any subsequent state water claims. In the context of the Colorado River, where Arizona's rights were established in 1922 (Compact) and 1963 (Supreme Court), tribal rights dating to 1860s-1880s reservation establishment are senior to the entire state water system.

This is not academic. As Colorado River shortage tiers deepen and state allocations are cut, the question of how tribal senior rights interact with shortage management becomes central to Arizona's water future. The Navajo Nation's unresolved water rights claim — affecting Upper Basin allocations — adds another layer of complexity.

The 22 Federally Recognized Tribes of Arizona

Navajo Nation (Diné Bikéyah)
Largest reservation in the U.S.: 17.5 million acres across Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah. ~175,000 enrolled members, ~170,000 residents on reservation. Government: Navajo Nation Council (88 delegates, 24 chapters). Headquarters: Window Rock, AZ. Water rights: UNRESOLVED — major pending claim against Arizona, New Mexico, and the federal government. Gaming: ~25 gaming enterprises. Coal mining on Black Mesa historically significant; transitioning to renewable energy.
Hopi Tribe
Located within the Navajo Nation (historically contested land). 2,531.8 sq miles. ~14,000 enrolled members. Oldest continuously inhabited settlements in North America (Oraibi village, ~1100 CE). Government: Hopi Tribal Council. Headquarters: Kykotsmovi, AZ. The Hopi-Navajo land dispute (1974-1996) resulted in one of the largest forced relocations of Native Americans since the 19th century, affecting ~12,000 Navajo who lived on disputed "Joint Use Area."
Gila River Indian Community
Located south of Phoenix in Maricopa and Pinal Counties. 372,000 acres. ~22,000 enrolled members (Akimel O'odham/Pima and Maricopa peoples). Headquarters: Sacaton, AZ. 2004 Water Settlement: 653,500 acre-feet per year — the largest water allocation in Arizona state history. Desert Diamond Casinos major revenue source. The Gila River tribes are the direct descendants of the Hohokam civilization that built Phoenix's ancestral canal network.
Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community
Located east of Scottsdale/Mesa metro. 52,600 acres. ~10,000 enrolled members. Headquarters: Scottsdale, AZ. 1988 Water Settlement: 122,000 acre-feet annually. Located in the Phoenix metro area, the SRPMIC is one of the most economically active tribal communities in Arizona, with significant gaming, commercial real estate, and development revenues. Adjacent to major tech corridors — Talking Stick Resort and entertainment complex.
Tohono O'odham Nation
4th-largest reservation in the U.S.: 2.8 million acres in southwestern Arizona + Mexico. ~34,000 enrolled members. Headquarters: Sells, AZ. The reservation shares 75 miles of the U.S.-Mexico international boundary — the only tribe in the contiguous U.S. with a reservation crossed by an international border. Traditional land was divided by the 1853 Gadsden Purchase. 2010 Water Settlement: 66,000 acre-feet. Desert Diamond Casinos major gaming revenue. Significant conflict with CBP over border surveillance on O'odham land.
White Mountain Apache Tribe
Fort Apache Reservation, 1.67 million acres in eastern Arizona. ~15,000 enrolled members. Headquarters: Whiteriver, AZ. Forest management, Sunrise Park ski resort, Fort Apache Historic Park. The White Mountain Apache Tribe is one of the largest timber operations among U.S. tribal nations. Significant water rights in the Salt River watershed. Apache county seat of government with jurisdiction over large portions of eastern Arizona federal lands.
San Carlos Apache Tribe
San Carlos Indian Reservation: 1.83 million acres in eastern Arizona. ~15,000 enrolled members. Headquarters: San Carlos, AZ. Rich in minerals and natural resources — the reservation contains significant copper deposits including the controversial Resolution Copper Mine (Rio Tinto/BHP partnership) that has faced intense opposition over destruction of Oak Flat (Chi'chil Biłdagoteel), a sacred site. Water rights in the Gila River system.
Fort McDowell Yavapai Nation
24,680 acres northeast of Scottsdale in Maricopa County. ~1,000 enrolled members. Headquarters: Fort McDowell, AZ. Fort McDowell Yavapai Casino — major revenue source. Pivotal in the history of Indian gaming in Arizona: the tribe's resistance to state demands in 1992 (threatening to withhold gambling equipment from the state) was a flashpoint in national tribal gaming politics. Water rights in the Verde River.

Gaming — The Economic Engine

Indian gaming in Arizona is a multi-billion dollar industry regulated by compacts between tribal governments and the State of Arizona under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA, 1988). Key data:

  • 25+ tribal gaming facilities in Arizona operated by multiple tribes
  • Revenue sharing: Arizona tribal gaming compacts require revenue sharing payments to the state — used for education, wildlife, tourism, and problem gambling treatment. Annual payments exceed $100 million.
  • Employment: Arizona tribal gaming employs approximately 15,000 people, predominantly non-tribal citizens — making tribes among the largest private employers in some rural communities
  • Phoenix metro gaming cluster: Salt River Pima-Maricopa, Gila River IC, Fort McDowell Yavapai, and Ak-Chin all operate major gaming facilities within an hour of downtown Phoenix, competing with each other and with out-of-state destinations
  • Class II vs Class III gaming: Compact restrictions in Arizona historically limited Class III gaming (including slot machines and table games). Compact renegotiations in 2002 and subsequent years have expanded Class III eligibility.

The Colorado River Indian Tribes Water Rights Controversy

The Colorado River Indian Tribes (CRIT), whose reservation straddles the Arizona-California border along the Colorado River, hold water rights of approximately 719,248 acre-feet annually — rights they have historically not fully used, leaving them as a water "bank" in the system. As Colorado River shortages deepened, CRIT became central to a controversial policy question: could unused tribal water be leased or sold to water-stressed Arizona and California communities?

In 2022-2023, the Biden Administration reached a deal with CRIT to voluntarily reduce their diversions by approximately 100,000-150,000 acre-feet per year in exchange for federal funding, helping to stabilize Lake Mead levels. This set a precedent for compensating tribes for water conservation that will shape all future shortage management agreements. The CRIT's water bank has become one of the most valuable assets in the Colorado River system.

The Oak Flat Controversy

Oak Flat (Chi'chil Biłdagoteel in the Western Apache language) is a sacred site in the Tonto National Forest about 70 miles east of Phoenix, used for centuries by the San Carlos Apache and Western Apache tribes for religious ceremonies, coming-of-age rituals, and burial practices. It contains thousands of archaeological sites.

In 2014, a provision was inserted into the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) by Arizona Senators John McCain and Jeff Flake — at the last minute, without hearings — that directed the transfer of Oak Flat from the U.S. Forest Service to Resolution Copper Mining (a joint venture of Rio Tinto and BHP). The mine project would use "block cave" mining that would cause Oak Flat to collapse into a 1.8-mile-wide, 1,000-foot-deep crater, destroying the site permanently.

The Apache tribe sued. Federal courts have issued conflicting rulings. As of 2024, the transfer was blocked pending further environmental review. The case involves the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA), the National Historic Preservation Act, and the treaty rights of the San Carlos Apache Tribe. It represents one of the most consequential pending decisions affecting indigenous religious rights in American history.

The Navajo Nation Water Crisis

The Navajo Nation has approximately 175,000 residents, many of whom live without running water — a remarkable figure for a U.S. population in 2024. Approximately 30% of Navajo Nation residents lack access to running water, relying on water hauling from watering points. This is the legacy of a century of federal water management that prioritized non-tribal users over the nation with the most senior water rights in the basin.

The COVID-19 pandemic (2020) exposed this crisis nationally when the inability to consistently wash hands contributed to the Navajo Nation having one of the highest per-capita infection rates in the country in spring 2020. Federal emergency funding provided some relief, but the structural water access deficit persists.

The Navajo Nation's pending water rights settlement — currently in negotiation — would provide the legal framework and funding for water infrastructure development on the reservation. Until settled, the Nation holds water rights it cannot practically exercise.
