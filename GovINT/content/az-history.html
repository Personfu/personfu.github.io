Arizona: A History of Conflict
Territory, War, Internment, Nuclear Testing, and the Politics of the Frontier
VERIFIED PUBLIC RECORD DOSSIER

The Deep Background

Arizona became an American territory through conquest. It became a state through political maneuvering. It became relevant to the modern world through its geography, its climate, and its position on the frontier of empire — first Spanish colonial, then Mexican, then American. Every significant issue in contemporary Arizona — water wars, border politics, military presence, indigenous sovereignty, election controversies — traces a direct line back to decisions made in the 19th century.

This is the history that explains the present.

Before Arizona: Spanish and Mexican Eras

Indigenous Peoples

Before any European set foot in what is now Arizona, the land was home to some of the most sophisticated indigenous civilizations in North America:

  • Hohokam people (300 BCE–1450 CE) — Built an elaborate canal system in the Salt River Valley covering 500+ miles of irrigation channels that supported a population of possibly 40,000-50,000 people in the Phoenix basin. They disappeared or dispersed around 1450 CE, likely due to climate change, drought, and social disruption. The canals they built are the direct predecessors of modern Phoenix irrigation infrastructure — some literally follow the same routes.
  • Ancestral Puebloans (Anasazi) — Occupied the Colorado Plateau region (northern Arizona) in complex cliff dwelling societies. Organized into what became modern Hopi, Zuni, and related Pueblo communities.
  • Apache peoples — The Ndé (Apache) arrived in the Southwest from Canada around the 14th-15th century and established themselves as formidable militarized raiders and traders. Multiple bands including Chiricahua (southeastern AZ), Western Apache (central AZ), and Jicarilla became the dominant military force in the region by the 18th century.
  • Tohono O'odham (Desert People) — Occupied the Sonoran Desert for thousands of years. Related to the Hohokam. Their traditional homeland straddles what is now the U.S.-Mexico border.
  • Navajo Nation (Diné) — Occupied the Colorado Plateau and developed a pastoral culture incorporating sheep (introduced by Spanish) into an existing hunter-gatherer-agricultural economy.

Spanish Colonial Period (1540–1821)

Francisco Vásquez de Coronado led the first major Spanish entrada into present-day Arizona in 1540-1542, searching (unsuccessfully) for the Seven Cities of Gold (Cíbola). The Spanish established missions in the Sonoran Desert — Tumacácori and San Xavier del Bac (Tucson) — as part of the mission system extended from New Spain (Mexico). The Spanish never fully controlled Arizona; Apache resistance prevented permanent settlement north of Tucson.

Mexican Sovereignty (1821–1848)

When Mexico achieved independence from Spain in 1821, Arizona became part of the Mexican state of Sonora. Mexican settlement in the region was minimal — a few ranches, the pueblo of Tucson, and mission communities. Apache raids made settled life dangerous and the Mexican government's reach was limited.

The Gadsden Purchase & Arizona Territory

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848)

The Mexican-American War (1846-1848) ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, under which Mexico ceded more than half its territory to the United States. This cession included California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona, and parts of Colorado, Wyoming, and New Mexico. The Rio Grande became the boundary. Tucson was still in Mexico.

The Gadsden Purchase (1853)

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo left a problem: the preferred southern route for a transcontinental railroad — through the flat Sonoran Desert — was still in Mexico. Secretary of War Jefferson Davis (later President of the Confederacy) commissioned diplomat James Gadsden to negotiate acquisition of the territory. The Gadsden Purchase of 1853 added 29,670 square miles to the United States for $10 million, including Tucson and all of southern Arizona south of the Gila River.

The purchase moved the border south, placing the Tohono O'odham people's traditional homeland in two countries — a division that affects their sovereignty and daily life to this day.

Arizona Territory — February 24, 1863

President Abraham Lincoln signed the Organic Act creating the Arizona Territory on February 24, 1863. The timing was not coincidental: the Civil War had created urgent need to organize western territories to deny them to Confederate influence and secure their mineral wealth (gold and silver had been discovered in Arizona). The Territory was carved out of the western portion of New Mexico Territory.

Prescott was the first territorial capital. The capital moved to Tucson in 1867, then back to Prescott in 1877, then to Phoenix in 1889 — a reflection of which population centers held political power at different moments. Phoenix, founded in 1867 on the site of Hohokam canal ruins, grew rapidly and secured the permanent capital designation.

The Apache Wars (1849–1886)

The most sustained and bloody conflict in Arizona's history lasted nearly four decades and involved the U.S. Army, Mexican Army, civilian militias, Apache scouts (Apache fighting Apache), and the various Apache bands led by some of the most tactically sophisticated guerrilla commanders in military history.

Cochise and the Bascom Affair (1861)

The immediate trigger for the most intense phase of the Apache Wars was the Bascom Affair of February 1861. Lieutenant George Bascom accused Chiricahua Apache leader Cochise of kidnapping a rancher's stepson (a claim historians consider likely false — the boy was probably taken by Pinal Apache). Bascom invited Cochise and his family to parley at Apache Pass (near present-day Willcox, AZ) then attempted to arrest them. Cochise escaped by cutting through a tent. Several of his relatives were captured and later hanged. Cochise retaliated by taking hostages; the Army executed his relatives; Cochise killed the hostages.

For the next decade, Cochise and the Chiricahua Apache waged relentless war across southern Arizona, frequently raiding into Mexico and back. Only a peace agreement negotiated by General Oliver Howard in 1872 — granting the Chiricahua a large reservation in southeastern Arizona's Dragoon Mountains, Cochise's homeland — ended Cochise's campaign. Cochise died in 1874.

Geronimo's War (1876–1886)

After Cochise's death and the dissolution of the Chiricahua Reservation in 1876 (forcing the band onto the San Carlos Reservation, which they despised), a new generation of Apache leaders emerged. Geronimo (Goyaałé, "One Who Yawns"), a Bedonkohe Apache medicine man and war leader, became the most famous.

Geronimo escaped the San Carlos Reservation multiple times, leading raids across Arizona, New Mexico, and northern Mexico. His final campaign (1885-1886) involved fewer than 40 warriors who evaded a force of 5,000 U.S. soldiers, 500 Mexican soldiers, and hundreds of Apache scouts for over a year — one of the most remarkable military campaigns in history of either side.

On September 4, 1886, Geronimo surrendered to General Nelson Miles at Skeleton Canyon in the Peloncillo Mountains, approximately 65 miles south of present-day Willcox, Arizona. He was the last major armed resistance leader to surrender in the contiguous United States.

The Apache were not allowed to stay in Arizona. Geronimo and the Chiricahua Apache — including those who had served as Army scouts — were loaded onto trains and transported to Florida (Fort Pickens, Fort Marion), then Alabama, then Oklahoma's Fort Sill, where they were held as prisoners of war for 27 years. Geronimo died at Fort Sill in 1909, never having been allowed to return to Arizona.

Tombstone and Wyatt Earp (1879–1882)

Silver was discovered near present-day Tombstone in 1877 by Ed Schieffelin, who was warned he'd find nothing but his tombstone in Apache country. The town that grew around the strike became one of the most famous places in American mythology — and one of the most revealing windows into how law, violence, commerce, and politics intersected in frontier Arizona.

The Context

Tombstone in 1880-1882 was a boomtown of 8,000-10,000 people in Cochise County, Arizona Territory. It was also the center of a struggle between two factions:

  • The Earps and their allies (Wyatt, Virgil, Morgan Earp; Doc Holliday): Republican, pro-law enforcement, aligned with mining and commercial interests, and politically connected to Prescott-based territorial governor John Fremont
  • The Cowboys (Ike and Billy Clanton, Frank and Tom McLaury, Johnny Ringo, Curly Bill Brocius): Loosely organized cattle rustlers and Democratic-aligned ranchers, with political protection from Cochise County Sheriff Johnny Behan

The O.K. Corral Gunfight — October 26, 1881

The gunfight that became the most famous moment in Western American mythology lasted approximately 30 seconds in a vacant lot adjacent to the O.K. Corral on Fremont Street, Tombstone. Three Cowboys (Billy Clanton, Frank and Tom McLaury) were killed. Virgil Earp was wounded in the leg. Morgan Earp was wounded in the shoulder. Wyatt Earp and Doc Holliday were uninjured.

The gunfight was immediately controversial. County Sheriff Johnny Behan arrested Wyatt and Virgil Earp for murder. A 30-day preliminary hearing ended with Justice Wells Spicer finding the Earps and Holliday acted in their official capacity; no charges were filed.

What followed was worse: Virgil Earp was ambushed in December 1881 and permanently crippled in his left arm. Morgan Earp was assassinated while playing billiards in March 1882. Wyatt Earp's subsequent "Vendetta Ride" — extrajudicial killings of suspected participants in Morgan's murder — resulted in murder charges that were never prosecuted. Wyatt left Arizona and never returned permanently.

Arizona Statehood — February 14, 1912

Arizona was the last of the contiguous 48 states to enter the Union, on Valentine's Day 1912. Statehood was delayed for decades due to federal concerns about Arizona's population size, its constitution (President Taft initially vetoed statehood because Arizona's constitution allowed recall of judges — he considered it too radical), and political dynamics in Congress.

The final push: Arizona removed the judge recall provision from its constitution, secured statehood, then immediately voted to add judge recall back in. The new state's early political character was strongly Democratic (reflecting the Confederate-sympathizing element of territorial settlers from Texas and the South), progressive, and labor-friendly — a legacy of the mining industry's conflicts with capital.

World War II — Arizona as Training Ground and Prison

The Military Transformation

World War II transformed Arizona more than any event since the Apache Wars. The combination of open space, clear skies, year-round flying weather, and proximity to the Pacific theater made Arizona the nation's premier military training location:

  • Luke Field (Luke AFB) — Named after Medal of Honor recipient Frank Luke Jr. (WWI Arizona pilot known as the "Balloon Buster"), became the largest advanced flight training base in the U.S. Over 16,000 fighter pilots trained here for WWII, including many of the Air Corps' top aces
  • Davis-Monthan — Then an Army Air Corps base, served as a B-24 and B-29 training facility
  • Williams Field — Advanced pilot training
  • Falcon Field (Mesa) — Trained Royal Air Force pilots under Lend-Lease arrangements
  • Yuma Army Air Field — Gunnery training for P-38, P-47 pilots
  • Fort Huachuca — Trained the segregated 92nd and 93rd Infantry Divisions
  • Gila Bend Army Air Field — Bombing range operations

Japanese American Internment in Arizona

Following Executive Order 9066, signed by President Roosevelt on February 19, 1942, approximately 120,000 Japanese Americans were forcibly removed from the West Coast and incarcerated in inland detention camps. Two of the ten main internment camps were in Arizona:

Poston War Relocation Center (Colorado River)
Located on the Colorado River Indian Reservation near Parker, AZ — on land leased from the tribal government over the objection of many tribal members. Three separate camps: Poston I, II, and III. Peak population: 17,814, making it the third-largest "city" in Arizona during 1942-1945. 10,000 acres of desert farmland. Internees built their own facilities in 120-degree summer heat. The federal government used internee labor to develop irrigation infrastructure that was later turned over to the tribe — a deeply complicated legacy.
Gila River War Relocation Center
Located on the Gila River Indian Community reservation near Sacaton, AZ — again on tribal land. Two camps: Canal and Butte. Peak population: 13,348. Also featured agricultural development using internee labor. The Gila River IC would later be one of the plaintiffs in landmark water rights litigation — their legal standing strengthened in part by their relationship with the federal government over the internment.

The internment was later acknowledged as a gross violation of constitutional rights. The Civil Liberties Act of 1988, signed by President Reagan, provided $20,000 reparations to surviving internees and issued a formal apology. Fred Korematsu's Supreme Court case (Korematsu v. United States, 1944), which upheld the internment, was formally vacated in 2018.

Nuclear Testing — Fallout Over Arizona

From 1951 to 1962, the United States conducted 100 atmospheric nuclear tests at the Nevada Test Site (NTS), located 65 miles northwest of Las Vegas and approximately 300 miles from Phoenix. The prevailing winds carried radioactive fallout east — directly over Utah, Nevada, and Arizona. The populations downwind became known as "Downwinders."

Operation Upshot-Knothole (1953)

The same nuclear test series that provided cover for the claimed Kingman UFO crash of May 1953 (see Kingman Crash dossier) involved 11 detonations at NTS between March and June 1953. Several shots produced fallout that reached Arizona. The "Dirty Harry" shot on May 19, 1953, produced the heaviest fallout of any NTS test, with detectable radioactivity reaching as far as Albany, New York.

The Downwinder Legacy

  • The Radiation Exposure Compensation Act (RECA) of 1990 created a compensation program for downwinders who developed specific cancers
  • Arizona counties covered: Apache, Coconino, Navajo, Yavapai, Gila, Maricopa (partially)
  • Studies have documented elevated cancer rates in downwind communities, though establishing direct causation is methodologically complex
  • The 2022 expansion of RECA eligibility extended coverage to more Arizona counties and cancer types
  • The Navajo Nation was also affected by uranium mining contamination — uranium ore mined on Navajo land fueled the weapons program, leaving contaminated mine tailings that have caused ongoing health problems for tribal communities

Barry Goldwater — The Arizona Senator Who Changed American Politics

Barry Morris Goldwater (1909–1998) is arguably the most consequential political figure in Arizona history. His 1964 presidential campaign, though a landslide defeat (he lost to LBJ 486-52 in the Electoral College), created the ideological framework for the modern conservative movement and laid the organizational groundwork for Ronald Reagan's 1980 victory.

Military Service

Goldwater was an Air Force officer and aviator throughout his adult life. He flew ferry missions during WWII, rising to colonel in the Air Force Reserve. He was a brigadier general in the Air Force Reserve when he ran for president, the highest-ranking officer to run for the presidency since Dwight Eisenhower. He flew 165 different aircraft types over his career — a record matched by very few civilian politicians.

Goldwater's military service and Senate Armed Services Committee work directly shaped Arizona's defense posture. He secured key military installations for Arizona including Luke AFB's continued existence during post-WWII drawdowns, and his relationships with military leadership influenced the positioning of intelligence and defense infrastructure throughout the state.

The 1964 Campaign — "In Your Heart You Know He's Right"

Goldwater's campaign represented the first serious challenge to the New Deal consensus that had governed American politics since 1932. His positions — opposition to the Civil Rights Act (on federalism and property rights grounds, though he had personally supported civil rights in Arizona), support for aggressive nuclear posture toward the Soviet Union, hostility to the welfare state — shocked mainstream Republican establishment but electrified a generation of conservative activists.

Johnson's campaign responded with the famous "Daisy" ad — a little girl counting flower petals cut to a nuclear countdown — that aired exactly once but became the most famous political advertisement in American history. It defined Goldwater as dangerous. He lost 44 states. His defeat was so complete that pundits declared conservatism dead.

They were wrong. The networks Goldwater built — the Young Americans for Freedom, the Western conservative infrastructure, the donor lists — became the foundation of the Reagan coalition that won the presidency 16 years later. Goldwater's campaign is widely credited as the beginning of the political realignment that made the South Republican and the urban Northeast Democratic.

The Goldwater-Nichols Act (1986)

Goldwater's most durable legislative achievement was the Goldwater-Nichols Department of Defense Reorganization Act of 1986, co-authored with Representative Bill Nichols. The law fundamentally restructured U.S. military command after the failures of the Iran hostage rescue (1980) and the Grenada invasion (1983), where inter-service coordination was catastrophically poor.

Goldwater-Nichols created the system of Unified Combatant Commands (USCENTCOM, USSOUTHCOM, INDOPACOM, etc.) where a single geographic commander — a four-star from any service — has full operational authority. It elevated the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs as the principal military advisor to the President. It required military officers to serve in joint assignments to be considered for promotion to general or flag officer. It is considered the most important military organizational legislation since the National Security Act of 1947 and remains in force today.

Barry Goldwater Range — the 2.7 million acre restricted airspace/military use area in southwestern Arizona — was renamed in his honor.

SB 1070 — "The Show Me Your Papers Law" (2010)

On April 23, 2010, Arizona Governor Jan Brewer signed Senate Bill 1070 (the Support Our Law Enforcement and Safe Neighborhoods Act), which became the most controversial immigration enforcement law in the United States and sparked a national debate about state authority over immigration that reached the Supreme Court.

The Law's Provisions

  • Required law enforcement officers to check immigration status of anyone stopped for another offense if there was "reasonable suspicion" they were unlawfully present
  • Made it a state crime for immigrants to fail to carry their immigration documents
  • Made it a state crime for undocumented workers to solicit, apply for, or perform work
  • Authorized state law enforcement to arrest individuals without warrants if there was probable cause they had committed any public offense that made them removable from the United States

The Supreme Court — Arizona v. United States (2012)

The Obama Administration sued Arizona, arguing SB 1070 was preempted by federal immigration law. The Supreme Court issued a mixed ruling in June 2012:

  • Struck down: The criminal penalty for failure to carry documents, the employment provision, and the warrantless arrest provision — finding these preempted by federal law
  • Upheld (for now): The "reasonable suspicion" check provision — the court allowed it to remain but noted it could still be struck down later if it led to unconstitutional profiling
  • Justice Kennedy: "The National Government has significant power to regulate immigration" — establishing that states cannot create parallel immigration enforcement regimes
