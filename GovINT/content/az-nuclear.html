Arizona's Nuclear Geography

Arizona occupies a peculiar position in American nuclear history: it was downwind of the atmospheric nuclear test site, above the uranium deposits that fueled the weapons program, and today hosts the nation's largest nuclear power plant. The nuclear age touched Arizona at every level — the weapons, the fallout, the fuel, and the power — and left consequences that continue to affect communities, water supplies, and health outcomes decades later.

Uranium Mining — Fueling the Manhattan Project and Cold War

The Navajo Nation Uranium Belt

Uranium was discovered in the Colorado Plateau — primarily on and around the Navajo Nation — in the 1940s. Beginning in 1944, the U.S. government's Manhattan Engineer District (then the Atomic Energy Commission after 1946) aggressively recruited miners to extract uranium ore for the weapons program. Between 1944 and 1986, approximately 30 million tons of uranium ore were mined from the Colorado Plateau region, with the Navajo Nation as the epicenter.

The Navajo men who worked in the mines were not told about the health risks of radiation exposure. Many did not speak English and relied on Navajo-speaking foremen for safety information — information that was often not provided. The mines were inadequately ventilated. Radon gas accumulated. Workers ate their lunches in the mines and tracked radioactive dust home on their clothes. Their families were exposed. Their children played in the tailings piles that remained when the mines closed.

Key facts about the uranium mining legacy:

  • 1,000+ abandoned uranium mines remain on or near the Navajo Nation, many never remediated
  • Groundwater contamination: Uranium, radium, and other radioactive elements have leached into groundwater used for drinking and livestock. Some wells on the Navajo Nation show uranium levels exceeding EPA standards by 100x
  • Health outcomes: Studies documented dramatically elevated lung cancer rates among Navajo uranium miners — men who were dying of a disease that was rare among Navajo people before the mining era. The AEC had data on radon exposure risks from the 1950s that was not shared with the miners.
  • Cold War contractor liability: The major mining companies — Kerr-McGee, Anaconda, Vanadium Corporation of America — were operating under AEC contracts that provided them legal immunity from radiation-related claims. The federal government essentially indemnified them against liability for worker health.

Radiation Exposure Compensation Act (RECA) — 1990

Congress passed the Radiation Exposure Compensation Act in 1990, creating a compensation program for:

  • Uranium miners who developed specific cancers
  • Downwinders — people who lived in specific areas near the Nevada Test Site and developed specific cancers
  • Atmospheric test participants ("onsite participants") who were present at nuclear tests

RECA provided $100,000 per uranium miner (or survivor), $75,000 per downwinder, and $75,000 per test participant. The law required applicants to prove their presence in specific geographic zones and their diagnosis of a listed cancer — requirements that excluded many legitimate claimants who couldn't document their location decades earlier or had the wrong cancer type.

2022 RECA Expansion: The RECA Amendments Act of 2022 expanded covered areas and cancer types, including:

  • Extended downwinder zones to include additional Arizona counties and all 50 states for weapons transport routes
  • Added additional cancer types to the covered list
  • Extended coverage to 1971 uranium miners (previous cutoff was 1971 for some categories)
  • The legislation was championed by the Arizona congressional delegation and passed with bipartisan support in the Senate — unusual for nuclear policy legislation

Nevada Test Site Fallout — The Atmospheric Testing Era (1951–1963)

The Tests

From January 27, 1951, to July 17, 1962, the United States conducted approximately 100 atmospheric nuclear tests at the Nevada Test Site (NTS, now the Nevada National Security Site), located in Nye County, Nevada, approximately:

  • 65 miles northwest of Las Vegas
  • 230 miles northwest of Phoenix
  • 300 miles from Tucson
  • 100 miles from Kingman, AZ

The prevailing winds in the region blow northeast and east — directly toward Utah, Nevada, Arizona, and beyond. The AEC chose the NTS in part because the downwind population was sparse: the Great Basin desert and the rural Southwest were considered acceptable risk zones. Military planners explicitly calculated the health risk to downwind populations and determined it was acceptable for national security purposes.

Operation Upshot-Knothole (Spring 1953) — The Arizona Connection

Operation Upshot-Knothole, conducted March–June 1953, included 11 detonations ranging from small "Dirty Harry" tactical weapon designs to thermonuclear device tests. Several shots in this series produced fallout that reached Arizona in measurable quantities:

  • Shot Simon (April 25, 1953): 43-kiloton yield. Produced the most contaminated cloud in the Upshot-Knothole series. Fallout track passed directly over Arizona.
  • Shot Harry / "Dirty Harry" (May 19, 1953): 32-kiloton yield. Notoriously "dirty" (high fallout per kiloton yield). Fallout detected as far as Albany, New York — indicating a continental-scale deposition track that necessarily passed through Arizona.
  • Shot Climax (June 4, 1953): 61-kiloton yield. The largest test in the series. The fallout track was east-southeast — directly over northern Arizona.

This is the same test series during which the alleged Kingman UFO crash occurred on May 20, 1953 — the day after Shot Harry. The proximity of the nuclear test detonation (100 miles from Kingman) to the alleged crash date has been noted by researchers examining the event. Whether classified nuclear-related aircraft were operating in Arizona airspace during this period is not publicly documented.

Film Badge Dosimetry Data — Hidden in Plain Sight

The AEC collected radiation dosimetry data from film badges worn by military personnel present at NTS tests, from civilian dosimeters in nearby communities, and from aerial survey aircraft. Some of this data was classified and only declassified decades later. Key document collections:

  • Nuclear Testing Archive (NTA) at the National Security Technologies (NSTec) facility maintains declassified records on all NTS tests
  • Defense Threat Reduction Agency (DTRA) maintains declassified test reports
  • NCI (National Cancer Institute) published a landmark 1997 study on I-131 (radioactive iodine) exposures across the continental U.S. from atmospheric testing — showing that every county in the United States received some I-131 fallout

Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station — Today's Nuclear Arizona

The Facility

Located 55 miles west of Phoenix near Tonopah, Arizona, Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station is the largest nuclear power plant in the United States by electricity generation. Operated by Arizona Public Service (APS) as operating agent, it is jointly owned by several utilities:

  • APS: 29.1%
  • Salt River Project: 17.49%
  • El Paso Electric: 15.8%
  • Southern California Edison: 15.8%
  • Public Service Company of New Mexico: 10.2%
  • Los Angeles Department of Water and Power: 5.7%
  • Southern California Public Power Authority: 5.9%

Three pressurized water reactors (PWRs): Units 1 (1986), 2 (1986), 3 (1988). Total licensed power: 3,937 megawatts, supplying electricity to approximately 4 million people in Arizona, California, New Mexico, and Texas.

The No-Water Plant

Palo Verde is unique in global nuclear history: it is the only nuclear power plant in the world that is not located adjacent to a river, lake, or ocean. All other nuclear plants use large bodies of water for cooling their condensers. Palo Verde instead uses treated municipal wastewater — sewage effluent from the Phoenix metropolitan area — delivered via a 36-mile pipeline from the 91st Avenue Wastewater Treatment Plant.

The plant consumes approximately 20 billion gallons of treated wastewater per year. This makes Palo Verde directly dependent on Phoenix metro sewage output — a dependency that creates both operational resilience (wastewater doesn't dry up) and a unique vulnerability (contamination of the wastewater supply could affect plant cooling). The water is treated to cooling tower quality standards before use.

License Renewals and Future Operations

  • Initial licenses: 40 years from first criticality
  • First renewal (2011): NRC approved 20-year license extensions for all three units, extending operation to 2045-2047
  • Second renewal consideration: APS has engaged in preliminary discussions about subsequent license renewal (SLR), which could extend operation to 2065-2067 — making Palo Verde potentially an 80-year operating plant
  • Arizona's decarbonization needs: As Arizona's renewable portfolio grows (solar is enormous due to sun exposure) and natural gas plants face pressure from carbon pricing, Palo Verde's baseload, zero-carbon generation has become increasingly valued

Security Posture

Palo Verde maintains one of the largest armed security forces of any nuclear plant in the United States. Key security elements (public information):

  • Physical protection plan: Required by NRC regulations (10 CFR 73.55), kept as "Safeguards Information" (SGI) — classified at a level below Secret but not publicly released
  • Armed security officers: Palo Verde's security contractor maintains a force capable of responding to a Design Basis Threat (DBT) attack — a force that could defeat a specific adversary profile defined by the NRC post-9/11
  • FAA flight restriction: 10-nautical-mile radius "national security no-fly zone" around Palo Verde — TFR KPVR. Enforcement by FAA and coordinated with DHS/USAF
  • Cyber protection: 10 CFR 73.54 applies; details in the companion Arizona Cyber dossier
  • Emergency planning zones: 10-mile emergency planning zone (EPZ) covering parts of Maricopa, Pinal, and Pima counties; 50-mile ingestion pathway zone. Maricopa County Emergency Management maintains radiation emergency plans.

The Grand Canyon Uranium Rush — Havasupai and Canyon Mine

The Colorado Plateau uranium deposits extend to the Grand Canyon region. Canyon Mine (also known as the EZ-1 breccia pipe), located approximately 6 miles south of the Grand Canyon's South Rim, is a uranium deposit that has been at the center of legal battles for decades:

  • Originally permitted in the 1980s for uranium mining
  • Mining halted in 1992 due to low uranium prices
  • Uranium prices rose in the 2000s, prompting Energy Fuels Resources to resume development
  • The Havasupai Tribe (Havasupai = "people of the blue-green water") consider the breccia pipe area sacred and part of their ancestral homeland; they have consistently opposed mining
  • A 2012 Obama Administration ban on new uranium mining claims on 1 million acres adjacent to Grand Canyon NP was challenged by the uranium industry
  • 2023: The Biden Administration formalized a 20-year mineral withdrawal on approximately 1 million acres of northern Arizona public land surrounding the Grand Canyon, blocking new uranium mining claims. Canyon Mine and other pre-existing valid claims are exempt from the withdrawal — meaning existing operations can continue.
  • The uranium at Canyon Mine would drain via groundwater pathways to Havasu Creek and potentially to the Colorado River — the water source for 25 million Americans downstream

Water Contamination Risk

Uranium mining near the Colorado River watershed represents a qualitatively different risk than the Cold War-era uranium mining on the Colorado Plateau. Lake Mead receives drainage from the Colorado River across the entire Upper Basin. Contamination of tributary streams from uranium mining operations — either through tailings leachate, groundwater migration, or spills — could affect the water supply for Arizona, Nevada, California, and Mexico.

Existing contamination from historical mines already affects some Arizona and Navajo Nation groundwater. The Cameron area (Navajo Nation) has documented uranium contamination in water supplies used by residents who lack access to municipal water systems.
