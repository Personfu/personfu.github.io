Overview — The Laboratory for Future Surveillance

Arizona's 372-mile border with Mexico is the most heavily surveilled non-military zone in the United States and arguably in the Western Hemisphere. Layers of technology — towers, drones, sensors, aerostats, satellites — create a persistent awareness of movement that no physical barrier alone could replicate. What is deployed here today will be replicated across international borders, urban policing corridors, and contested zones worldwide tomorrow.

Understanding Arizona's border surveillance apparatus is understanding the future of state power. The technology companies that won contracts here — and some that lost them — shape the next generation of surveillance systems globally. The failures are as instructive as the successes.

The SBInet Failure — Boeing's $1 Billion Lesson (2006–2011)

Before the current technology infrastructure existed, DHS awarded Boeing a $1 billion "Secure Border Initiative" (SBInet) contract in 2006 to build a "virtual fence" along the entire Southwest border. The project became one of the most expensive technology failures in U.S. government history.

  • Boeing integrated radar, cameras, and communications into networked towers — the Integrated Surveillance Intelligence System (ISIS)
  • The towers suffered from high false-alarm rates, poor performance in desert dust and rain, software integration failures, and inadequate communication bandwidth
  • DHS Secretary Janet Napolitano cancelled the program in January 2011 after spending $1 billion for a 53-mile pilot section that never performed reliably
  • GAO criticized DHS for poor acquisition management and inadequate testing before deployment
  • The failure fundamentally shaped how CBP approached technology procurement going forward: smaller contracts, technology-neutral competitions, emphasis on proven components

Integrated Fixed Towers (IFT) — Elbit Systems

The Contract

In 2014, CBP awarded Elbit Systems of America — the U.S. subsidiary of Israeli defense contractor Elbit Systems — a $145 million contract (later expanded) to deploy Integrated Fixed Towers (IFTs) in the Tucson Sector. As of 2023, approximately 55 IFT towers are operational along the Tucson Sector border, representing the most sophisticated fixed-tower surveillance system deployed by a U.S. agency.

Technical Specifications

  • Detection range: Radar detects movement up to 7.5 miles from the tower in ideal conditions; effective range in operational use typically 3-5 miles
  • Radar type: Solid-state, pulsed-Doppler ground surveillance radar capable of classifying moving targets (person vs. vehicle vs. animal)
  • Cameras: High-definition EO (electro-optical) and IR (infrared) cameras with automatic slewing to track radar contacts
  • Tower height: 140-foot fixed towers; some locations use 80-foot portable configurations
  • Communications: Encrypted microwave backhaul to centralized Remote Video Surveillance System (RVSS) command facilities
  • Autonomous tracking: Once radar detects a contact, cameras automatically track the target; sensor operator at RVSS facility can observe and direct agent response
  • Power: Grid power where available; solar/generator hybrid at remote locations

Elbit Systems — Who They Are

Elbit Systems is an Israeli defense electronics company headquartered in Haifa with approximately 17,000 employees worldwide. Their technology has been tested in one of the most operationally challenging border environments in the world: the Israeli-Palestinian and Israeli-Lebanon borders. CBP's selection of Elbit represented a deliberate choice to leverage battle-tested Israeli border technology.

Elbit Systems of America maintains its headquarters in Fort Worth, Texas, and holds multiple U.S. government contracts beyond IFT, including UAS systems, training systems, and avionics. Their parent company trades on NASDAQ (ESLT).

Remote Video Surveillance Systems (RVSS)

The RVSS is the fixed camera network predating IFTs. While IFTs have long-range radar and are tower-mounted, RVSS cameras are primarily deployed along roads, crossing points, and high-traffic areas near ports of entry. RVSS camera feeds aggregate at Border Patrol stations where agents monitor displays and vector responding units.

The Tucson Sector RVSS network includes hundreds of cameras providing overlapping coverage of priority corridors. Camera technology has been upgraded multiple times since initial deployment in the late 1990s; current systems include low-light and thermal imaging for 24-hour operation.

Autonomous Surveillance Towers (AST)

The AST program, launched in 2020, represents the next generation beyond IFTs. Developed by Anduril Industries (founded by Palmer Luckey, creator of Oculus VR), ASTs are significantly cheaper than IFTs ($20,000-$60,000 per unit vs. $1M+ per IFT tower), rapidly deployable, and incorporate AI-powered detection that runs entirely at the tower with no bandwidth-intensive video streaming required.

  • AI classification: On-board compute runs machine learning models that classify targets as human, vehicle, or animal in real-time, generating alerts only for human targets
  • Mesh networking: ASTs form self-organizing mesh networks, relaying contact data tower-to-tower to maintain communications in terrain where backhaul is unavailable
  • Rapid deployment: Can be set up by two people in under an hour; solar-powered models operate independently for weeks
  • Contract scale: CBP awarded Anduril a $250 million contract in 2022 for AST deployment across multiple sectors
  • Arizona deployment: Significant AST deployment in Tucson and Yuma sectors supplementing IFT coverage

MQ-9 Predator B (Guardian) — CBP Air & Marine Operations

CBP's Air and Marine Operations (AMO) operates a fleet of MQ-9 Guardian drones — the maritime/border variant of the USAF's MQ-9 Reaper — for long-endurance surveillance of the Arizona-Mexico border. The Guardian differs from the military Reaper in that it carries no weapons and is equipped with a maritime sensor package (AN/AAS-44 EO/IR and synthetic aperture radar).

CBP MQ-9 Operations in Arizona

  • Operating base: Tucson Air Branch at Davis-Monthan AFB (co-located with USAF 355th Wing)
  • Flight time: MQ-9 can loiter for 27 hours at altitude, providing persistent awareness that no crewed aircraft can match
  • Coverage area: A single MQ-9 mission can surveil hundreds of miles of border corridor continuously
  • Crew: Pilot and sensor operator at ground control station; missions can be relayed to ground crews in real time
  • Data sharing: MQ-9 sensor data is shared with Border Patrol agents on the ground, CBP command centers, and when warranted, with DEA and military intelligence commands
  • Fleet size: CBP operates approximately 10 MQ-9 aircraft across the U.S., with multiple allocated to the Southwest border

Ground Sensors — Buried Intelligence

Thousands of ground sensors are buried along known crossing routes in the Tucson and Yuma sectors. They represent the oldest continuous technology layer in border surveillance:

  • Unattended Ground Sensors (UGS): Seismic sensors detect footsteps; magnetic sensors detect vehicles; some units include acoustic detection
  • Tunnel Detection System (TDS): Network of sensors designed to detect underground tunneling — a countermeasure to the proliferation of drug and migration tunnels under high-security areas like Nogales
  • Integration: Sensor alerts trigger RVSS camera review and agent dispatch, creating a layered response system
  • Battery life: Modern units operate for 1-3 years before requiring maintenance

The Tohono O'odham Nation — Sovereignty in the Surveillance Zone

The Tohono O'odham Nation's 2.8 million acre reservation stretches 75 miles along the international boundary with Mexico. The border cuts through their traditional homeland, separating tribal members who are U.S. citizens from those who are Mexican citizens — a border that was imposed across their territory when the Gadsden Purchase (1853) made southern Arizona part of the United States.

The surveillance infrastructure raises specific sovereignty concerns for the Tohono O'odham:

  • Physical wall vs. technology: The Nation has officially opposed physical wall construction through their reservation, preferring technology-based surveillance as less disruptive to traditional land use and cross-border community connections
  • IFT towers on tribal land: Some IFT towers are located on O'odham land under agreements negotiated with the Tribal government. The terms of these agreements — how data is shared, how CBP agents can operate on tribal land — are not fully public
  • Drug smuggling impact: The remote terrain and international boundary create significant pressure from cartels using O'odham land as a crossing corridor; the tribal community is directly affected by both smuggling and the law enforcement response
  • Tohono O'odham Police Department: Maintains its own law enforcement capacity; coordinates but also sometimes conflicts with Border Patrol over jurisdiction and approach

GAO Oversight — Accountability Mechanisms

The Government Accountability Office (GAO) has audited CBP border technology programs extensively. Key reports:

GAO-22-105082: Border Surveillance Technology
2022 GAO report evaluating CBP's surveillance technology portfolio including IFT, RVSS, and AST. Assessed cost-effectiveness, coverage gaps, and integration challenges. Found CBP lacks metrics to assess whether surveillance technology is achieving its goals.
GAO-20-432: SBInet Lessons Learned
Post-mortem analysis of the Boeing SBInet failure. Found DHS did not adequately define requirements, conduct realistic testing, or maintain contractor accountability. Required reading for understanding how CBP reformed technology acquisition.
GAO-23-105720: CBP Air & Marine Operations
Review of CBP AMO MQ-9 operations effectiveness, mission hour data, and coordination with Border Patrol ground units. Includes cost-per-apprehension analysis and sensor data sharing protocols.
DHS: Border Wall System Program Overview
Official DHS overview of all physical and technology components: wall segments, access roads, lighting, sensors, and camera systems by sector. Includes Arizona sector-specific data.

Civil Liberties Concerns — ACLU and Privacy Law

The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) and Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) have raised sustained concerns about border surveillance technology:

  • 100-mile rule: CBP claims broad authority to conduct warrantless searches and surveillance within 100 miles of any U.S. border or international coast. Two-thirds of the U.S. population lives in this zone. Nearly all major Arizona cities (Phoenix, Tucson, Yuma) fall within it.
  • Facial recognition deployment: CBP uses facial recognition at ports of entry for traveler identification. The extension of facial recognition to surveillance technology farther from the border is a subject of ongoing litigation and policy debate.
  • Scope creep: Technology originally deployed for immigration enforcement is used for drug interdiction, criminal fugitive tracking, and — post-9/11 — counterterrorism. Civil liberties advocates argue this scope creep is not authorized and lacks oversight.
  • Data retention: MQ-9 surveillance footage, sensor logs, and camera records are retained for varying periods. FOIA requests for specific retention policies have produced inconsistent responses.
  • ACLU v. DHS: Multiple ACLU cases challenging CBP surveillance authorities and the constitutionality of the 100-mile zone doctrine.
