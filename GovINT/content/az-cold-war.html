The Manhattan Project & Arizona's Role (1942–1945)

While the Manhattan Project's famous sites (Los Alamos, Hanford, Oak Ridge) lay outside Arizona, the state provided critical uranium ore sourced from Navajo Nation mines that fed the project's material pipeline. The Navajo Tribal Council in 1950 officially banned uranium mining on the reservation — too late to prevent the health catastrophe documented in the decades that followed.

The Trinity Test (July 16, 1945, White Sands, NM — 200 miles from Arizona's border) was the world's first nuclear detonation. Radioactive fallout from Trinity drifted northeast and north; some fallout tracks passed through New Mexico and the broader Southwest. The AEC did not publicly acknowledge Trinity-related fallout health effects until decades later.

Davis-Monthan AFB was designated a B-29 Superfortress base in 1944-1945 and played a direct logistical role in the Pacific strategic bombing campaign that preceded and followed the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. By 1946 D-M hosted the largest B-29 force in the world before they were mothballed — the genesis of what would become AMARG (the "Boneyard").

Key Arizona Cold War Installations
  • Davis-Monthan AFB (Tucson): Strategic Air Command 1948-1992. B-29, B-36, B-47, B-52 basing. First jet-powered SAC base in Arizona. 355th Strategic Reconnaissance Wing. Now AMARG boneyard and A-10 training hub.
  • Luke AFB (Glendale): Fighter pilot training factory since 1941. Cold War: F-86, F-100, F-105, F-4 training. "Fighter Pilot Capital of the World." Retooled for F-16/F-35 in post-Cold War era.
  • Williams AFB (Chandler): Premier jet training base 1941-1993. Produced more than 26,000 pilots during Cold War. Closed under BRAC 1993; now Phoenix-Mesa Gateway Airport.
  • Fort Huachuca EPG (1954): Electronics Proving Ground — electromagnetic isolation in remote Huachuca Mountains enabled radar, ECM, and early digital systems testing.
  • Titan II ICBM Silos (Tucson Basin): 18 silos in a ring around Tucson, 1963–1984. Most powerful nuclear weapon ever deployed by the U.S. military.
Titan II ICBM: Arizona's Nuclear Deterrent (1963–1984)

From 1963 to 1984, 18 Titan II Intercontinental Ballistic Missile silos were operationally deployed in a ring around Tucson, making Arizona one of the most nuclearized landscapes in America. Each Titan II carried a single W53 thermonuclear warhead with a yield of 9 megatons — the largest yield of any missile ever deployed by the United States, approximately 600 times the yield of the Hiroshima bomb.

Technical Specifications
Titan II (SM-68B / LGM-25C)
Height: 103 feet (31m). Diameter: 10 feet. Weight: 330,000 lbs fueled. Fuel: Aerozine-50 (50% UDMH / 50% hydrazine) + NTO oxidizer — hypergolic, no ignition required, stored in missile. Range: 9,000+ miles. Accuracy (CEP): 0.8 nautical miles. Time to launch: ~58 seconds. Warhead: 1 × W53 (9 Mt yield) on a Mk-6 reentry vehicle.
Accidents & Close Calls
Air Force Historical Record
September 19, 1980 — Damascus, Arkansas: Titan II silo accident. A worker dropped a socket wrench striking the missile, rupturing the fuel tank. The resulting explosion killed one airman, injured 21, blew the 740-ton silo door 200 feet, and ejected the W53 warhead (which did not detonate due to safety mechanisms). This was the most serious U.S. ICBM accident. Arizona's 18 silos used the same propellant system.
Titan Missile Museum
Green Valley, AZ — NRHP Landmark
Site 571-7 (Complex 571-7) at Green Valley, 25 miles south of Tucson, is the only preserved Titan II silo in the world — now a National Historic Landmark and Smithsonian affiliate. The actual silo, launch control center, and partially disarmed Titan II are preserved in the condition they were in when decommissioned under the START treaty process. Open to the public with tours.

The 18 Arizona silos were assigned to the 390th Strategic Missile Wing at Davis-Monthan AFB under Strategic Air Command (SAC). Each silo required a two-person launch crew in the underground launch control center (LCC). Launch required simultaneous key turns by both crew members (the "two-man rule") — both keys had to be turned within 2 seconds and maintained for 5 seconds to launch.

The Titan IIs were deactivated between 1982-1984 under the Reagan administration's decision to replace aging liquid-fueled missiles with solid-fueled Minuteman III and MX Peacekeeper missiles. The deactivation was also driven by the Damascus accident and recognition of the hypergolic fuel's catastrophic accident risk. All 17 non-museum Arizona silos were destroyed under treaty obligations and their fuel complexes decontaminated.

Strategic Air Command (SAC) at Davis-Monthan

Davis-Monthan was among the founding Strategic Air Command installations when SAC was established on March 21, 1946 under General George Kenney. Under General Curtis LeMay's leadership (SAC commander 1948-1957), D-M became a critical node in America's nuclear deterrence posture.

B-36 Peacemaker Era (1949–1958)
Convair B-36
The B-36 "Peacemaker" was the first truly intercontinental bomber — capable of reaching Soviet targets from U.S. bases without refueling. Six piston engines plus four jets ("six turning, four burning"). Range: 10,000+ miles. 75,000 lbs payload. Arizona's desert climate was ideal for its massive 230-foot wingspan operations. D-M hosted the 43rd and 303rd Bombardment Wings flying B-36s during the height of the early nuclear bomber deterrent.
B-47 Stratojet Era (1951–1965)
Boeing B-47
The B-47 was the first swept-wing jet bomber and entered service with SAC in 1951. At D-M, the 303rd Bomb Wing flew B-47s on continuous alert — aircraft loaded with nuclear weapons, crews ready to launch within minutes. "Chrome Dome" airborne alert missions flew crews and weapons over the Arctic toward Soviet targets, ready to release if ordered. Arizona sited B-47 nuclear weapon storage in hardened "igloos" at D-M.
B-52 Stratofortress (1955–Present)
Boeing B-52H
D-M B-52 units transferred to other SAC bases as the Titan II wing took priority at D-M. However, D-M became the primary B-52 STORAGE facility — AMARG's climate-controlled desert environment preserves stored B-52s in flyable condition. Under the START I treaty (1991), Russia demanded and received the right to satellite-observe B-52 destructions at D-M — sawed fuselages visible from orbit confirmed treaty compliance.
SIGINT & Intelligence Infrastructure

Arizona's Cold War intelligence infrastructure extended beyond conventional military bases into signals intelligence and electronic warfare facilities that remain partially active today:

  • Ft. Huachuca SIGINT (NSA): The Electronic Proving Ground developed expertise in signals exploitation that later became core to NSA's presence on post. The NSA/CSS Arizona site (popularly attributed to the San Xavier area south of Tucson) operates under NSA authority. Details remain classified.
  • Auxiliary Field 6 / Libby Army Airfield: Fort Huachuca's airfield supports SIGINT collection aircraft (RC-12 Guardrail family) operated by Army intelligence units trained at USAICoE.
  • HAARP Predecessor Research: University of Arizona and affiliated federal labs participated in ionospheric research during the Cold War period.
  • Nike Missile Sites: Phoenix was defended by Nike Ajax (later Hercules) surface-to-air missiles in the late 1950s, including nuclear-armed Nike Hercules variants capable of destroying Soviet bomber formations. Deactivated by 1970s.
Nuclear Testing Fallout & Arizona (1951–1963)

The Nevada Test Site (NTS), located 65 miles northwest of Las Vegas and approximately 230 miles from Phoenix, conducted 100 atmospheric nuclear tests between 1951 and 1963. Arizona was downwind of the majority of these tests, receiving measurable radioactive fallout across the state.

Operation Upshot-Knothole (1953)
11 Tests, Spring 1953
Shot Harry ("Dirty Harry") — May 19, 1953: 32-kiloton yield. One of the most contaminating shots in NTS history. Fallout cloud tracked northeast across Utah (St. George UT famously hit hard) and east toward Arizona's northern tier and beyond to Albany, NY. Film badge dosimetry confirmed significant deposition. Shot Simon — April 25, 1953: 43 kt, fallout cloud produced one of the most geographically widespread contamination patterns of the entire atmospheric test program.
Operation Plumbbob (1957)
29 Tests, May–October 1957
The largest atmospheric test series at NTS. Included HOOD (July 5, 74 kt — largest atmospheric test in continental U.S. history), PRISCILLA (37 kt), and 27 others. Total yield: ~278 kt. Troop maneuvers during tests exposed thousands of soldiers to radiation. Fallout patterns from multiple 1957 shots tracked through Arizona. The NCI 1997 nationwide I-131 study found measurable thyroid dose exposures in every U.S. county from Plumbbob tests.
The RECA Compensation Program
Public Law 101-426 (1990) & Amendments
The Radiation Exposure Compensation Act (RECA, 1990) provides lump-sum compensation to downwinders in specific geographic zones who developed certain cancers attributable to NTS fallout. Arizona counties in the original RECA zone: Coconino, Mohave, Navajo, Apache, Yavapai. The RECA Amendments Act of 2022 expanded coverage to additional Arizona counties and additional cancer types, with increased compensation to $150,000 for downwinders. The program was extended through 2024 as Congress debated further expansion.

The 1997 National Cancer Institute (NCI) report — Estimated Exposures and Thyroid Doses Received by the American People from Iodine-131 in Fallout Following Nevada Atmospheric Nuclear Bomb Tests — is the definitive public health document on NTS fallout. It found that Americans born between 1951-1971 were exposed to I-131 from fallout. Northern and western Arizona received measurable I-131 deposition, with thyroid doses estimated for children who consumed local milk from cows grazing on fallout-contaminated pasture.

The Defense Threat Reduction Agency (DTRA) maintains the Nuclear Test Personnel Review (NTPR) program providing dose reconstructions for military veterans who participated in atmospheric tests. Nuclear test veterans may qualify for VA healthcare and compensation for radiation-related illness under the 2023 PACT Act expansion.

Barry Goldwater & Cold War Politics

Senator Barry Goldwater (R-AZ) was among the most consequential Cold War political figures produced by Arizona. His 1964 presidential campaign against Lyndon Johnson defined the right flank of American Cold War politics and permanently realigned the Republican Party.

  • Military Background: Air Force Reserve Brigadier General. WWII ferry pilot (transported B-17s across the Atlantic). Early Cold War advocate for air power and nuclear deterrence. Member Senate Armed Services Committee for decades.
  • 1964 Campaign: Accepted the Republican nomination at the Cow Palace, SF. His statement "extremism in the defense of liberty is no vice" and remarks about using tactical nuclear weapons in Southeast Asia enabled LBJ's "Daisy Ad" (nuclear explosion imagery). Lost 44 states, 27 million to 43 million popular votes.
  • Long Legacy: His campaign mobilized the conservative grassroots movement that elected Reagan 16 years later. Goldwater-Nichols Defense Reorganization Act 1986: restructured the entire U.S. military, created unified combatant command system, elevated Chairman JCS role — arguably the most important defense reform since 1947.
  • Barry M. Goldwater Range: Named for him — 2.7M acres of military training airspace southwest of Phoenix, largest contiguous military airspace complex in the U.S.
Korean & Vietnam Era — Arizona Casualties

Arizona contributed significantly to both the Korean War (1950-1953) and Vietnam War (1964-1975), with substantial casualties recorded:

  • Korean War: 4,268 Arizona residents served; 152 killed in action. The Arizona National Guard's 158th Infantry Regiment ("Bushmasters"), veterans of WWII Pacific combat, was reactivated for Korea.
  • Vietnam War: 958 Arizona residents killed in action. The Arizona Vietnam Veterans Memorial at Wesley Bolin Plaza in Phoenix names all KIA. Luke AFB trained thousands of F-100/F-105/F-4 pilots who flew combat missions over Vietnam; Davis-Monthan A-7 Corsair II units also flew Vietnam combat tours.
  • MIA/POW: Arizona had 33 service members listed as Missing In Action or Prisoner of War from Vietnam. The National League of POW/MIA Families chapter in Arizona was among the most active nationally in the 1970s-80s advocacy campaigns.
  • Agent Orange Exposure: Significant numbers of Arizona Vietnam veterans were exposed to herbicide agents. The 2022 PACT Act substantially expanded VA disability presumptions for toxic exposure.
Civil Defense & CONELRAD

Arizona communities participated in the federal civil defense apparatus throughout the Cold War, including programs designed to protect civilians from Soviet nuclear attack:

  • CONELRAD (1951-1963): Control of Electromagnetic Radiation. All AM radio stations would go off-air during an attack except designated stations at 640 and 1240 kHz. Phoenix stations KOOL and KRUX were designated CONELRAD stations. Replaced by Emergency Broadcast System (EBS) in 1963.
  • Fallout Shelter Program: Phoenix City Hall, the Arizona State Capitol, and hundreds of commercial buildings were designated fallout shelters under the Federal Civil Defense Administration. Yellow-and-black shelter signs still visible in older Phoenix buildings.
  • Operation Alert (1954-1961): Annual nationwide civil defense drills. Arizona participated with evacuation route designations out of Phoenix toward the White Mountains and high desert — away from the Titan II silo ring and the direction of Soviet attack trajectories.
  • FEMA Region 9: Arizona's Cold War-era civil defense structures evolved into the modern FEMA Region 9 (Western U.S.) emergency management framework headquartered in Oakland, CA.
The Defense Economy: How Cold War Military Spending Built Modern Arizona

Cold War military spending was arguably the single largest driver of Arizona's post-WWII economic transformation. Arizona's population grew from 499,261 (1940 census) to 1,302,161 (1960) to 2,718,425 (1980) — a 445% increase in 40 years. Military and defense-related economic activity was central to this growth.

Motorola Defense Electronics
Scottsdale/Phoenix, 1949–Present
Motorola established its Government Electronics Division in Scottsdale in 1949 to manufacture military communications equipment. The division grew into a major employer — developing the first handheld walkie-talkie (SCR-536), military radios, satellite communications systems, and early electronic warfare systems for the U.S. military. Eventually spun off as Freescale Semiconductor (2004), then acquired by NXP Semiconductors (2015). Motorola's choice of Phoenix helped seed Arizona's technology industry.
Raytheon Missile Systems
Tucson, 1951–Present
Raytheon Missile Systems (now RTX / Raytheon Missiles & Defense) has operated its primary missile manufacturing facility in Tucson since 1951. The Tucson campus produces Patriot, Tomahawk, Standard Missile, AIM-120 AMRAAM, AIM-7 Sparrow, TOW, and dozens of other missile systems. Raytheon is Tucson's largest private employer (~15,000 workers). The campus was originally selected due to proximity to Davis-Monthan AFB and the wide-open desert airspace for missile testing.
General Dynamics (Convair) — FY Atlas
San Diego–Tucson Pipeline
General Dynamics Convair Division manufactured the Atlas ICBM (first U.S. ICBM, operational 1959) in San Diego. While Convair's primary facility wasn't in Arizona, the Atlas ICBM bases included Atlas-F sites in southern Kansas and elsewhere. The Titan II manufactured by Martin Marietta drew upon components from Arizona suppliers including Motorola and Garrett AiResearch (Phoenix). Garrett manufactured the Titan II's ground support power systems and environmental control systems.
