The Kingman UFO Incident
Alleged Craft Recovery Near Kingman, Arizona — May 1953
DISPUTED FOLKLORE

The Claim

On or about May 21, 1953, according to the central account in this case, an extraordinary event allegedly occurred in the Mohave Desert near Kingman, Arizona. The claim originates with a single primary source: an engineer who used the pseudonym "Fritz Werner" but was later identified as Arthur Stansel, a project engineer who had worked on atomic bomb testing during Operation Upshot-Knothole at the Nevada Test Site.

Stansel's account, as recorded by researcher Ray Fowler in the 1970s, states the following: On the date in question, Stansel and approximately 30 other personnel — engineers and scientific personnel — were transported from Phoenix to an undisclosed desert location by bus, with the windows blacked out and under strict instructions not to discuss what they were about to see. Upon arrival in the desert, they were escorted under armed guard to a site where a craft had apparently impacted the ground.

Stansel described the object as approximately 30 feet in diameter, oval or disc-shaped, with a metallic surface that showed no visible seams, rivets, or fasteners. The surface material appeared undamaged despite the apparent impact. His specific assignment was to calculate the speed at which the object had impacted based on analysis of ground deformation and impact stress on the craft's exterior — a task consistent with his engineering specialty.

Stansel further claimed that personnel at the site informed him that two occupants had been recovered from the craft — small humanoid beings, approximately 4 feet tall, wearing metallic suits. He stated he did not personally see the beings but was informed of their recovery by colleagues at the site. After completing his assigned calculation, Stansel was transported back to Phoenix by bus under the same blacked-out conditions and signed a non-disclosure agreement.

The Primary Source

The Kingman case rests almost entirely on the testimony of Arthur Stansel, filtered through researcher Ray Fowler's documentation in the 1970s. Fowler, a credentialed MUFON researcher and author of multiple UAP books, contacted Stansel and obtained his account. Stansel agreed to share his story on the condition that his real name not be publicly disclosed; he used the pseudonym "Fritz Werner." His identity as Stansel was eventually revealed through subsequent research.

A second corroborating voice emerged in the form of Bill Uhouse, an engineer who claimed to have worked on recovered craft propulsion technology in a classified setting. Uhouse made his claims in interviews and presentations in the 1990s, stating that he had worked at Wright-Patterson AFB on engineering derived from a recovered non-human craft. Uhouse never directly confirmed the Kingman incident specifically, but his claims of working on reverse-engineered propulsion placed him in a narrative proximity to cases like Kingman.

Neither Stansel nor Uhouse left behind verifiable documentary evidence of their claimed activities. No official records, personnel files, project documents, or corroborating witnesses have been identified that confirm either man's presence at or near a crash site. Both men are now deceased.

The sole official record linking Stansel to military atomic test work is his documented participation in Operation Upshot-Knothole at the Nevada Test Site in 1953 — the same year as the alleged Kingman incident. This has been cited by proponents as establishing his presence in the region and his clearance level. Skeptics note that it merely confirms he worked in the region's classified weapons program, not that he witnessed any craft recovery.

Military Context: May 1953

The date of the alleged Kingman incident — May 1953 — places it in one of the most militarily active periods in the American Southwest:

  • Operation Upshot-Knothole (March–June 1953) — A major nuclear weapons test series conducted at the Nevada Test Site (NTS), approximately 100 miles north of Kingman as the crow flies. The series involved 11 nuclear detonations over roughly three months. Engineers, physicists, and military personnel were rotated through the NTS in significant numbers. Classified convoys moving equipment and personnel between NTS, Los Alamos, Sandia, and secondary facilities were routine and not publicly documented.
  • Regional military logistics — The stretch of Route 66 passing through Kingman (connecting Needles, California with Flagstaff, Arizona) was a primary overland logistics corridor for military equipment moving between California facilities, Nevada test sites, and facilities further east. Unusual convoys, road closures, and military presence were not uncommon and not necessarily remarkable to local residents.
  • Mohave County population density — In 1953, the Mohave County population was a fraction of its current level. Kingman itself had fewer than 10,000 residents. Large sections of the desert between Kingman and the Nevada Test Site were effectively uninhabited. A classified ground operation in this terrain could plausibly be conducted with minimal civilian observation.
  • Air traffic over the region — The Cold War-era expansion of nuclear testing and aerospace development meant that classified experimental aircraft — including early jet designs and drone programs — regularly flew over the Nevada-Arizona corridor. Unidentified aerial objects were not necessarily non-human in origin; many historical UAP reports from this era are now attributable to classified American test programs.

Investigative History

The documentary record on the Kingman incident is defined primarily by absence rather than content:

  • Project Blue Book — The USAF's official UAP investigation program, which operated from 1952 to 1969, contains no file for the Kingman incident. Researchers have confirmed this absence through FOIA requests and review of the now-declassified Blue Book archive, available through the National Archives.
  • FOIA requests — Multiple FOIA requests directed at the Air Force, DoD, and intelligence agencies specifically seeking records related to an alleged May 1953 crash or recovery near Kingman, Arizona have returned either no responsive records or heavily redacted documents that contain no substantive corroboration. Rejections based on national security exemptions have been cited by believers as evidence of active concealment; skeptics note that FOIA non-response also frequently reflects non-existence of responsive records.
  • MUFON investigation — MUFON researchers have reviewed the case extensively. The consensus within the research community is that Stansel's account is internally consistent but unverifiable. Ray Fowler documented his findings in the book UFOs: Interplanetary Visitors (1974), which remains the primary published source on the case.
  • Stanton Friedman analysis — Nuclear physicist and UAP researcher Stanton Friedman examined the case and found Stansel's background credible while acknowledging that no independent confirmation exists. Friedman noted that an engineer with Stansel's profile would have been exactly the type of person tapped for such an assignment, if it had occurred.

The structural dynamic of the Kingman investigation mirrors other alleged crash-retrieval cases: the absence of official records is interpreted by proponents as evidence of a successful cover-up, and by skeptics as the most parsimonious explanation — that the event did not occur as described.

Mohave County Location

The geographic setting of the alleged Kingman incident is significant in several respects:

Kingman, Arizona sits at the junction of I-40 (the old Route 66 corridor) and US-93, which runs north toward Las Vegas and the Nevada Test Site. This crossroads position made Kingman a natural waypoint for anything moving between the Los Angeles basin, the Nevada desert, and the Four Corners region. In 1953, the logistics infrastructure for classified programs at NTS depended on this corridor.

Kingman Army Air Field (KAAF) — A significant WWII-era military installation that operated from 1942 to 1945 as a gunnery school, KAAF at its peak had more than 6,000 personnel and hundreds of aircraft. By 1953, the formal base had been closed and its assets dispersed, but the physical infrastructure — runways, hangars, support buildings — remained largely intact. The presence of functional but abandoned military infrastructure in the Kingman area created potential logistical capability for a classified ground operation without establishing a visible active military presence.

Terrain — The desert terrain surrounding Kingman — broken washes, low mountains, and vast flat bajadas — is ideal for concealing ground operations from aerial observation and minimizing civilian witness exposure. The visual horizon is limited by topographic features in most directions, and population centers are distant.

Relationship to Other Cases

The Kingman incident exists within a pattern of claimed southwestern desert crash-retrieval events that UAP researchers have studied as a potential cluster:

  • Roswell, NM (July 1947) — The most documented alleged crash-retrieval case. Involves debris recovery and alleged biological material recovery in the New Mexico desert, initially described as a "flying disc" by the Army Air Force before being reclassified as a weather balloon. Roswell is the foundational case for the crash-retrieval narrative.
  • Aztec, NM (1948) — An alleged recovery near Aztec, New Mexico, primarily attributed to fraudulent claims by Silas Newton and Leo GeBauer, thoroughly debunked by journalist Frank Scully's later-corrected account in Behind the Flying Saucers (1950). Most researchers treat Aztec as a hoax, which complicates Kingman by association.
  • Pattern analysis — Researchers examining the cluster of 1940s-1950s southwestern claims note several common elements: remote desert setting, classified military presence, official silence, single or few primary witnesses, and claims involving biological recovery. Critics note that these common elements are equally consistent with a recurring storytelling template as with recurring actual events.
  • Distinguishing factors — Unlike the Aztec case, Stansel's account does not appear to have been fabricated for personal profit, and his professional background is consistent with the role he describes. Unlike Roswell, no debris, contemporaneous military documentation, or mass civilian witness corroboration exists for Kingman.

Reliability Assessment

The Kingman incident is classified as DISPUTED in this wiki's reliability framework. The following factors inform that classification:

  • No physical evidence — No recovered material, no photographic documentation, no physical ground traces have been confirmed or independently verified from the alleged 1953 event.
  • No corroborating official records — Project Blue Book, FOIA records, and available government archives contain nothing confirming a May 1953 recovery near Kingman, Arizona. The silence of official records does not resolve in either direction.
  • Single primary witness, now deceased — Arthur Stansel's account cannot be further tested. Ray Fowler's documentation is the only direct source. The chain of custody for the information is limited.
  • Plausible background context — Stansel's documented employment history, his presence in the Nevada Test Site program, and the logistical realities of May 1953 in the region are all consistent with the possibility of a classified event, while confirming nothing.
  • Government UAP databases — AARO's historical review (Vol. 1, 2024) addresses various historical claims. Researcher accounts indicate that cases like Kingman are logged as historical claims without confirmation. AARO found no evidence of non-human crash retrieval programs in records it was able to access, which critics note may not include all relevant compartments.

The Kingman case is preserved in this wiki as a documented historical claim — a data point in the long arc of UAP disclosure that Congress is currently working to resolve — not as a confirmed event.
