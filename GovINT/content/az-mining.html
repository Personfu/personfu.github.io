The Copper Era: Building Arizona on Metal

Arizona's identity was built on copper as thoroughly as any place on earth. The "5 C's" (Cattle, Citrus, Climate, Cotton, Copper) schoolchildren learned for generations — and Copper was the one that built cities. Arizona produces approximately 68% of all U.S. copper and is consistently among the world's top copper-producing regions.

The Major Copper Deposits

Bisbee / Lavender Pit
Cochise County, 1877–1975
The Copper Queen Mine (later Phelps Dodge) made Bisbee the largest city in Arizona between Phoenix and El Paso from 1900-1920. Total production: ~8 billion pounds of copper, plus significant gold, silver, lead, zinc. The Lavender Pit open-cut mine visible today is 300 feet deep, 1,000 feet wide. 1917 Bisbee Deportation: Phelps Dodge and local law enforcement forcibly deported 1,286 striking miners (mostly IWW members, Mexicans, and other immigrants) by gunpoint into the New Mexico desert — one of the most dramatic instances of corporate suppression of labor in U.S. history.
Globe / Miami District
Gila County, 1876–Present
The Globe-Miami mining district in Gila County has operated for 150+ years with multiple distinct operations: Miami Copper Company (1910), Inspiration Copper (1916), and the Old Dominion mine. The district features a unique geology — the Miami fault system — producing exceptionally high-grade porphyry copper. Freeport-McMoRan and BHP operate active mines in the district today. The Pinto Valley Mine (BHP) produces approximately 130 million pounds of copper per year.
Morenci / Clifton
Greenlee County, 1872–Present
The Morenci mine (Freeport-McMoRan) is the largest copper mine in North America by production. Open-pit operation removed the entire original town of Morenci. Annual production: ~900 million pounds of copper. Employs approximately 3,600 workers in one of the most remote areas of Arizona. 1983 Strike: The Arizona copper strikes of 1983-1984 (Morenci, Ajo, Bagdad, Ray) were among the most bitter labor conflicts in late 20th century American history — Phelps Dodge hired permanent replacement workers and effectively destroyed union representation at AZ copper mines.
Ray / Asarco
Pinal County, 1911–Present
The Ray Complex (Asarco / Grupo Mexico subsidiary) includes the Ray open-pit copper mine and Hayden smelter (one of the last copper smelters operating in the U.S.). The Hayden smelter has been one of the largest single-point sources of sulfur dioxide (SO₂) air pollution in the country — leading to multiple EPA enforcement actions and consent decrees. 2016 EPA Superfund listing of adjacent areas due to historic arsenic deposition in Hayden and Winkelman towns.
Bagdad / Congress
Yavapai County
Bagdad Mine (Freeport-McMoRan) in Yavapai County is a significant porphyry copper-molybdenum deposit. Congress, AZ (near Yarnell) was a major gold mining boom town in the 1880s-1890s — the Congress Mine produced $8.5M in gold (1880s dollars) and at its peak had 500 residents before the 1920s bust. The Congress Mine ruins are now a historic site with active prospect claims.
Tombstone / Toughnut Mine
Cochise County, 1877–1911
Ed Schieffelin's 1877 silver discovery at Tombstone generated one of the West's most famous mining booms. The Toughnut and Contention mines produced an estimated $37M in silver (1880s dollars). The mines flooded below the water table by 1909 — pumping costs exceeded profits — killing the silver era. The town's violent boom-era culture (Earps, Cowboys, O.K. Corral) was directly tied to the wealth and lawlessness that accompanied sudden mining prosperity.
Uranium Mining Crisis (1944–Present)

Arizona's uranium legacy is among the most consequential environmental justice stories in American history. The Navajo Nation sits atop significant uranium deposits; the federal government aggressively mined those deposits from 1944-1986 with devastating public health consequences.

  • AEC Recruitment (1944): The Atomic Energy Commission recruited Navajo miners for uranium extraction, offering wages far above typical employment options. No health warnings were provided. AEC had internal data on radon lung cancer risk from 1947 onward but did not share it with miners or their families.
  • Vanadium Corp of America, Kerr-McGee, Anaconda: Primary mining contractors on Navajo land. Operations included Monument Valley, Cameron, Church Rock, Red Valley areas. Ore processed at mills in Shiprock, NM (abandoned tailings pile remained unenclosed for decades).
  • 1,200+ Abandoned Mines: After uranium prices collapsed and environmental regulations tightened in the 1980s, mining companies largely walked away. Over 1,200 abandoned uranium mines remain on Navajo Nation land; approximately 500+ are in Arizona (Cameron, Gap, Monument Valley area).
  • Church Rock Spill (1979): United Nuclear Corporation's tailings dam at Church Rock, NM failed July 16, 1979 — the same day as the Three Mile Island accident (which dominated national attention). Released 94 million gallons of radioactive liquid waste and 1,100 tons of tailings into the Puerco River, which flows into Arizona through the Navajo Nation. Largest radioactive spill in U.S. history.
  • Health Legacy: Studies by Indian Health Service and EPA document dramatically elevated lung cancer rates in Navajo communities near mines — 25x background rates in heavily exposed miners. Kidney disease from uranium in drinking water. Genetic effects in children of exposed miners documented.

The Labor Wars: Mining Strikes & Violence

Arizona's mining history is inseparable from its labor history — decades of violent conflicts between mining companies and workers that shaped Arizona's political culture, labor laws, and ethnic demographics.

Bisbee Deportation (1917)
July 12, 1917
During WWI copper demand surge, 1,286 miners — mostly IWW (Industrial Workers of the World) members, Mexicans, and other immigrants — were arrested by 2,000 Phelps Dodge-organized "Citizens' Protective League" members (deputized by Cochise County Sheriff Harry Wheeler). Miners were loaded into cattle cars at gunpoint and dumped in the New Mexico desert at Hermanas with no food or water. No criminal charges were ever brought against organizers. The event is among the most documented examples of corporate-government labor suppression in U.S. history. President Wilson created a federal mediating commission but deported miners were never compensated.
1983 Arizona Copper Strikes
July 1983 – February 1984
Following copper price crashes, Phelps Dodge demanded massive wage concessions from unions at Morenci, Ajo, Douglas, and Bisbee operations. When 13 unions (led by USW Local 616) refused, PD hired permanent replacement workers — then legal under Taft-Hartley. Arizona Governor Bruce Babbitt controversially deployed National Guard troops and DPS to protect strikebreakers. The strike was broken by 1984; unions were decertified. The 1983 strike effectively ended significant union representation in Arizona copper mining and demonstrated that permanent replacement of striking workers was viable in industrial settings — a template later used nationally.
Mexican Labor in Arizona Mining
1880s–Present
Mexican workers were essential to Arizona mining from the beginning but faced systematic discrimination. The "Mexican wage" — a lower pay scale than Anglo workers for identical work — was standard practice at most Arizona mines into the 1940s. Clifton-Morenci had a Mexican majority population but Anglo management and wage structure. The 1915 Arizona "Alien Labor Law" prohibited companies with 5+ employees from hiring more than 20% "aliens" — targeting Mexican workers who were often U.S. citizens by birth. The law was eventually struck down as unconstitutional but the racialized wage structure persisted until WWII-era labor shortage equalization.
Resolution Copper / Oak Flat Controversy (Current)

The most contentious current mining controversy in Arizona is Resolution Copper Mine, a proposed Rio Tinto / BHP joint venture copper mine beneath Oak Flat (Apache: Chi'chil Biłdagoteel) in the Tonto National Forest east of Superior, Arizona.

The Deposit
Porphyry Copper, 2,000ft Below Surface
Resolution is a massive porphyry copper deposit — estimated 40 billion pounds of copper — the largest undeveloped copper deposit in North America. The ore body lies at approximately 7,000 feet below surface, making traditional open-pit mining impossible. Resolution Copper proposes block cave mining: a technique where ore is blasted from below, collapses under gravity, and is extracted through tunnels. Block caving at this scale would create a subsidence crater approximately 1.8 miles wide and 1,100 feet deep on the surface — eliminating Oak Flat entirely.
The 2014 NDAA Land Swap
Section 3003, FY2015 NDAA
Section 3003 of the FY2015 National Defense Authorization Act (Dec 2014) — inserted as a last-minute rider by Senators John McCain (R-AZ) and Jeff Flake (R-AZ) without standalone committee vote — authorized the transfer of 2,400 acres of Tonto National Forest (including Oak Flat) to Resolution Copper in exchange for other federal lands. Oak Flat had been protected from mining since President Eisenhower's 1955 executive order. The land swap enabled mine permitting to proceed despite strong tribal opposition.
Apache Sacred Site Litigation
Apache Stronghold v. United States
The San Carlos Apache Tribe and allied groups filed Apache Stronghold v. United States (9th Circuit), arguing the land swap violates RFRA (Religious Freedom Restoration Act) and the Treaty of 1852 by destroying Chi'chil Biłdagoteel — a sacred site used for Apache coming-of-age ceremonies and spiritual practice for centuries. The 9th Circuit initially ruled 2-1 against Apache Stronghold (2023), but an en banc rehearing was granted. The case has implications for indigenous religious rights, federal trust obligations, and the limits of Congress's power to override sacred site protections.

The Biden administration's release of a Final Supplemental Environmental Impact Statement (FSEIS) in January 2021 (later rescinded and restarted) and the Trump administration's actions to fast-track the project illustrate how Resolution Copper has become a case study in the intersection of mining economics, indigenous rights, and executive branch priorities. Economic projections: $65B+ in economic value, 3,700 direct/indirect jobs over 40-year mine life. Environmental justice concerns: destroyed Apache sacred site, subsidence risk to adjacent communities.

Environmental Superfund Sites

Arizona's mining legacy has produced numerous EPA Superfund sites — many tied directly to mineral extraction:

  • Iron King Mine / Humboldt Smelter (Yavapai Co.): CERCLA NPL site. Lead, arsenic, cadmium contamination from historic gold/silver mining and smelting operations. Residential soil cleanup ongoing since 2013.
  • Tucson International Airport (TIA) Complex: Trichloroethylene (TCE) groundwater plume from former electronics/defense manufacturing (Hughes Aircraft, IBM, Burr-Brown, Air Force). One of the largest TCE plumes in U.S. groundwater — 13 miles long. EPA ROD issued 1986. Cleanup ongoing.
  • United Nuclear Corp (NM/AZ border): Uranium mill tailings from Church Rock spill. Cross-border contamination in Puerco River watershed through northeastern AZ (Navajo Nation).
  • Hayden/Winkelman (Pinal Co.): Arsenic-contaminated soil from ASARCO Hayden copper smelter historic emissions. EPA Superfund site; residential soil replacement program completed 2010s.
  • Phoenix-area groundwater plumes: Multiple VOC groundwater contamination plumes in Phoenix metro from historic industrial activity — Motorola 52nd Street (TCE), Hassayampa Superfund site (pesticides/solvents).

Grand Canyon Uranium Mining Controversy

The breccia pipe uranium deposits on the Kanab Plateau and South Rim area of the Grand Canyon represent some of the highest-grade uranium ore in the United States. They also lie within the most visited national park in America and within the Colorado River watershed — creating a multi-decade environmental and political controversy.

Canyon Mine / Pinyon Plain Mine
6 miles from South Rim
Energy Fuels Resources' Canyon Mine (renamed Pinyon Plain Mine in 2022 PR effort) is the most controversial active uranium mine in the U.S. Located 6 miles south of the Grand Canyon South Rim, the EZ-1 breccia pipe ore body contains uranium at concentrations 40-100x typical ore. A 1,000-foot shaft has been sunk. The mine holds a pre-existing valid mining claim exempt from the 2012 Obama-era and 2023 Biden-era mining bans on surrounding federal lands. Ore transport routes would pass through Tusayan, AZ — a Havasupai traditional travel corridor to Grand Canyon.
2023 Biden Grand Canyon Monument
August 8, 2023 Presidential Proclamation
President Biden established the Baaj Nwaavjo I'tah Kukveni – Ancestral Footprints of the Grand Canyon National Monument on August 8, 2023 — designating approximately 917,618 acres of federal land around Grand Canyon National Park. The monument prohibits new mining claims; existing valid claims (including Canyon Mine) remain unaffected. Havasupai, Hopi, Navajo, Zuni, and Southern Paiute tribes had long advocated for the monument. Arizona's Republican delegation (as of 2023) opposed the designation. Monument legal challenges pending from mining industry.
Groundwater Contamination Risk
USGS / EPA Risk Assessment
The primary environmental concern with Canyon Mine is breccia pipe geology: the ore bodies are vertical pipes that penetrate multiple aquifer layers including the regional Coconino Aquifer — the primary drinking water source for communities including Tusayan and multiple Havasupai springs. Any mine shaft penetrating the water table risks uranium contamination percolating into the aquifer. The Havasupai Tribe (whose reservation lies at the bottom of Havasu Canyon, with springs as their only water source) has been the most vocal opponent. USGS monitoring wells track water table impacts; uranium above drinking water standards (30 μg/L) has been detected in some springs near historic AZ breccia pipe mines.
