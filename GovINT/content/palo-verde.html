Station Overview & Unique Engineering

Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station (PVNGS) sits 55 miles west of Phoenix in the Sonoran Desert near Wintersburg, Arizona. It is the largest nuclear generating station in the United States by nameplate capacity and the only nuclear power plant in the world that does not draw cooling water from a river, lake, or ocean.

Instead, Palo Verde uses approximately 20 billion gallons per year of treated municipal wastewater — effluent from the Phoenix metropolitan area's 91st Avenue Wastewater Treatment Plant, conveyed via a 36-mile dedicated pipeline. This water is further processed in Palo Verde's own treatment facility before use in the evaporative cooling towers. The cooling water never contacts the reactor systems.

The plant consists of three Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR) units, each rated at approximately 1,314 MW gross / 1,270 MW net. Each unit uses a CE System 80 nuclear steam supply system (NSSS) from Combustion Engineering, now part of Framatome/Westinghouse lineage. The cores contain approximately 241 fuel assemblies each.

Construction History

  • NRC Construction Permits: 1976
  • Unit 1 First Criticality: January 1985 / Commercial Operation: June 1, 1986
  • Unit 2 Commercial Operation: September 1986
  • Unit 3 Commercial Operation: January 1988
  • Total construction cost: approximately $5.9 billion (1988 dollars)
  • Original licensed capacity: 1,270 MW/unit; uprated to ~1,314 MW/unit gross through NRC-approved power uprate programs

APS serves as the licensed operator (Operating Agent) for all three units. Each co-owner receives electricity proportional to ownership share and bears a proportional share of all costs including fuel, operation, maintenance, and decommissioning funding.

License Renewal History

Original operating licenses were issued for 40 years. Palo Verde applied for and received 20-year license renewals from the NRC in 2011:

  • Unit 1: Licensed through June 1, 2045
  • Unit 2: Licensed through April 24, 2046
  • Unit 3: Licensed through November 25, 2047

APS has publicly indicated interest in Subsequent License Renewal (SLR) — extending licenses an additional 20 years to 2065–2067. The NRC SLR process requires a new generic environmental impact statement review and updated time-limited aging analysis (TLAA). No formal SLR application had been filed as of 2025.

The NRC dockets all Palo Verde regulatory documents at ADAMS (Agencywide Documents Access and Management System) under facility docket numbers 05000528/529/530.

Power Output & Grid Role

Palo Verde's three units provide approximately 35% of Arizona's electricity generation capacity and export power to California, Nevada, and New Mexico through the Western Interconnection grid. Arizona is a net electricity exporter, and Palo Verde is a cornerstone of that export capacity.

Capacity factor: Palo Verde typically achieves 90%+ capacity factors — nuclear plants run nearly continuously at full power unlike gas or coal plants that cycle. In recent years Units 1-3 have collectively generated 32-33 TWh annually.

Grid operator: PVNGS connects to the Western Area Power Administration (WAPA) and Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC) regional transmission organization grid. The station has its own 500 kV switchyard interconnecting to multiple transmission corridors.

Drought and clean energy convergence: As Colorado River water shortages pressure hydropower (Glen Canyon Dam, Hoover Dam output reduced), Palo Verde's non-water-withdrawing nuclear output becomes increasingly critical to Southwest grid stability.

Security Classification

Palo Verde is subject to the most stringent physical security requirements in the U.S. civilian nuclear industry under 10 CFR Part 73:

  • Protected Area: Vehicle barriers, detection systems, armed response force
  • Vital Areas: Control rooms, fuel storage, safety systems — access via two-person integrity
  • Owner Controlled Area: Outer security perimeter, vehicle inspection
  • Armed Security Force: APS Protective Force (~200+ officers), trained to law enforcement standards
  • Design Basis Threat (DBT): NRC sets classified threat parameters; licensees must defeat DBT in force-on-force exercises
  • Force-on-Force Exercises: Triennial NRC-graded exercises simulating armed attack

FAA maintains a Temporary Flight Restriction (TFR) over PVNGS: 10 nautical miles radius, no aircraft below 18,000 ft MSL without authorization. Violators are intercepted by USAF/ANG aircraft under NORAD protocols.

Cybersecurity: 10 CFR 73.54 Requirements

The NRC's nuclear cybersecurity rule 10 CFR 73.54 (effective 2009) requires all operating nuclear power plants to establish, implement, and maintain a Cyber Security Program protecting against cyberattacks on systems and networks associated with safety, security, emergency preparedness (SSEP) functions.

Defense-in-Depth (DiD)
10 CFR 73.54(b)(1)
Multiple concentric security zones. Outermost (Level 4) IT business networks. Level 3 corporate/operational. Level 2 control system network. Level 1 direct control systems. Level 0 field devices (PLCs, RTUs). Each boundary requires security controls proportional to consequence.
Critical Digital Asset (CDA) Identification
10 CFR 73.54(a)(1)
Licensees must identify all CDAs — any programmable device that performs, supports, or is interconnected with SSEP functions. Once identified, CDAs must be protected from cyberattack. Asset inventory is itself security-sensitive information.
Air Gap Requirement (Safety Systems)
NEI 08-09 Rev 6
Safety-related Level 1 systems must be isolated via hardware data diodes or physical air gaps — no bidirectional network connections. This prevents the Stuxnet/Triton attack vector that targeted safety systems in other nations' nuclear facilities.
Insider Threat Program
10 CFR 73.56 / Fitness for Duty
Personnel reliability programs, behavioral observation, unescorted access authorization (UAA) reviews. Background investigations, psychological screening, drug testing. Two-person integrity for sensitive areas. Regular UAA reviews for behavioral changes.
Annual Cyber Drills & Assessments
NRC Inspection Procedure 71130.10
NRC cybersecurity inspectors conduct regular announced and unannounced inspections. Licensees must conduct periodic cyber vulnerability assessments and tabletop exercises. Inspection findings categorized by significance per NRC Inspection Manual Chapter 0609.
Triton/TRISIS Threat Relevance
CISA Alert ICS-ALERT-17-318-01A
The 2017 Triton/TRISIS malware specifically targeted Schneider Electric Triconex Safety Instrumented System (SIS) controllers — widely used across nuclear and process industries including U.S. plants. Russian attribution (CNIIHM). NRC issued information notice IN 2017-09 to licensees.
Fuel Cycle & Waste

Fuel: Uranium enriched to ~4-5% U-235. Fuel assemblies fabricated by Framatome (formerly AREVA) and Westinghouse. Each unit requires fresh fuel every 18-24 months per refueling outage.

Used Fuel Storage: Spent fuel is stored first in on-site spent fuel pools (SFP) for at least 5 years of cooling, then transferred to dry cask storage in the Independent Spent Fuel Storage Installation (ISFSI) on-site. As of 2025, Palo Verde has 100+ dry casks stored at its ISFSI.

Yucca Mountain: The intended permanent repository (NV) was defunded by Congress in 2011 after $15B in development. No permanent repository exists in the U.S. All spent fuel remains on-site at reactor stations including Palo Verde — essentially indefinitely until a solution is enacted by Congress.

Consolidated Interim Storage: NRC licensed two private interim storage facilities (Holtec in NM, ORANO in TX) but Congressional authorization and funding remain unresolved.

Decommissioning: APS maintains NRC-required decommissioning trust funds. Total projected decommissioning cost: $2.7B+ (all three units). 60+ years post-shutdown to SAFSTOR (safe storage) then dismantlement is standard path.

Emergency Planning (EPZ)

Federal regulations (10 CFR 50, Appendix E) require nuclear plants to maintain two emergency planning zones:

  • 10-Mile EPZ (Plume Exposure): Evacuation/shelter-in-place planning for radiation release. Covers western Phoenix metro suburbs, Buckeye, Goodyear areas. Population within 10-mile EPZ: approximately 50,000 (growing due to Phoenix suburban expansion).
  • 50-Mile EPZ (Ingestion Pathway): Food/water protection planning. Covers virtually all of Metro Phoenix (4.8M people), extending to Prescott, Yuma. ADHS coordinates with APS, Maricopa County, ADEQ for 50-mile planning.

FEMA evaluates adequacy of emergency plans independently of NRC. Palo Verde conducts biennial emergency exercises graded by NRC/FEMA. Arizona DPS, Maricopa County Sheriff, fire departments, and ANG helicopter assets are integrated into emergency response plans (ERPs).

Post-Fukushima (2011) Orders: NRC issued Mitigation Strategies Order EA-12-049 requiring all U.S. reactors to have FLEX (Flexible Coping Strategies) equipment — portable generators, pumps, batteries — to maintain core cooling during beyond-design-basis events including station blackout. Palo Verde implemented FLEX by 2016.

Significant NRC Events & Enforcement

Palo Verde has had several significant regulatory events over its operational history:

  • 1989-1993: NRC placed Palo Verde on "watch list" due to maintenance backlogs and quality assurance deficiencies. APS significantly reformed maintenance programs.
  • 2012: Unit 3 classified as "Column 4" in NRC Action Matrix (requiring enhanced NRC oversight) due to a series of equipment failures. Exited enhanced oversight 2014.
  • 2015: NRC issued yellow finding (higher significance) for Unit 1 containment issues. Required additional inspection and corrective action.
  • 2018-2019: Component cooling water system valve issues identified; replaced across all three units.
  • 2021: Unit 1 emergency diesel generator issues — backup power reliability is NRC's most scrutinized safety function post-Fukushima.

Despite these events, Palo Verde has maintained relatively good operational performance metrics. The NRC's public reactor oversight website publishes all performance indicator data quarterly at nrc.gov.

Threat Intelligence Nexus

Palo Verde sits at the intersection of multiple threat vectors that intelligence agencies actively monitor:

  • Physical Security: Post-9/11 DBT includes aircraft impact analysis. NRC required all plants to evaluate aircraft impact threats under 10 CFR 50.150 (2009). Hardened structures confirmed installed per NTTF.
  • Cyber Threats (State Actors): Volt Typhoon (China, AA23-144A) pre-positioned in U.S. energy infrastructure 2021-2023. Palo Verde as largest U.S. nuclear generator is a priority intelligence collection target for adversaries.
  • Supply Chain Risk: Digital instrumentation and control (DI&C) upgrades use foreign-manufactured components. NRC Order EA-11-153 requires supply chain security review for safety-related DI&C systems.
  • Drone Incursions: Since 2019 unidentified drones have been observed near multiple U.S. nuclear plants. NRC issued Information Notice 2019-08. APS armed security force has drone detection/interdiction authority.
  • Insider Threat: FBI and NRC coordinate on insider threat cases. Behavioral threat assessment teams (BTATs) at major plants.
  • Water Contamination: 20B gal/yr treated wastewater cooling dependency creates novel attack surface — contamination of cooling water supply (rather than reactor systems) as potential disruption vector.
Small Modular Reactors (SMR) & Arizona's Nuclear Future

The same Southwest grid pressures (coal retirement, drought-reduced hydro, growing demand from data centers and EV charging) driving interest in Palo Verde license extension are also driving interest in new nuclear construction in Arizona and the broader West.

NuScale Power — SMR Development
NRC Design Certification 2022
NuScale received the first-ever NRC design certification for an SMR in January 2023 — its VOYGR-6 (6 × 77 MWe modules). The Utah Associated Municipal Power Systems (UAMPS) Carbon Free Power Project (CFPP) at INL was a prospective deployment, though UAMPS cancelled in 2023 due to cost escalation. APS and SRP have observed SMR developments closely as potential Palo Verde successors.
Kairos Power — Hermes Reactor
NRC Construction Permit 2023
Kairos Power received an NRC Construction Permit in 2023 for Hermes, a 35 MWt fluoride salt-cooled high-temperature reactor (FHR) demonstration at Oak Ridge, TN. Google announced PPAs for future Kairos commercial reactors (2030s). Data center demand growth in Arizona makes SMR power purchase agreements increasingly attractive to hyperscalers.
Westinghouse AP1000 — New Build
Vogtle 3&4 Reference Design
The only new large reactors built in the U.S. in decades — Vogtle 3 (2023) and 4 (2024) in Georgia — demonstrated the enormous cost and schedule challenge of large-plant new construction. Vogtle project cost: $35B for two 1,117 MWe units vs. ~$6B initial estimate. This experience reinforces that SMRs and Palo Verde extension are more likely Arizona paths than new large-plant construction.
