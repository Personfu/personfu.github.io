The Fundamental Problem

Arizona exists at the intersection of two geological lies: a desert that appears fertile with irrigation and a river treaty built on fraudulent hydrology. The Colorado River Compact of 1922 allocated water that doesn't exist. The engineers who drew the compact measured the river during an anomalously wet period. The river's actual long-term average flow is approximately 12-13 million acre-feet per year — but the compact allocated 16.5 million, including Mexico's share. Seven states and a nation have spent 100 years consuming a check the river cannot cash.

For Arizona, the consequence is existential. Without engineered water delivery, the Phoenix metro — the fifth-largest city in the United States with 4.9 million people — could not exist. Tucson. Mesa. Scottsdale. Chandler. All built on borrowed water time. The reckoning is not coming. It has arrived.

Colorado River Compact — 1922

The Santa Fe Conference

On November 24, 1922, representatives of seven states met at Bishop's Lodge in Santa Fe, New Mexico, under the chairmanship of Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover. The problem was clear: California was developing rapidly and threatened to claim so much Colorado River water under "prior appropriation" doctrine that upstream states would be cut off. The compact was designed to prevent California from locking up all the water before the other states could use it.

The compact divided the Colorado River Basin into Upper and Lower:

  • Upper Basin (Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, New Mexico) — 7.5 million acre-feet annually
  • Lower Basin (California, Nevada, Arizona) — 7.5 million acre-feet annually
  • Mexico — 1.5 million acre-feet (Mexican Water Treaty, 1944)
  • Total allocated: 16.5 million acre-feet annually
  • Actual average river flow: ~12-13 million acre-feet

The hydrology was based on measurements from 1906-1921, which included some of the wettest years in the basin's recorded history. Tree-ring studies conducted decades later confirmed the compact was built on an outlier wet period.

Arizona Refuses to Sign

Arizona did not ratify the compact until 1944 — 22 years after the other states. Arizona's objection was not abstract: the state feared California would dominate the Lower Basin allocation, leaving Arizona with little water despite being the geographically largest Lower Basin state. Arizona also worried that if it ratified the compact, California would lock in its use before Arizona built infrastructure to actually use its share.

The state fought California for decades through litigation, lobbying, and outright confrontation. In 1934, Arizona Governor Benjamin Moeur actually mobilized the Arizona National Guard and sent a small fleet of ferryboats ("Arizona Navy") to the Colorado River to stop California from starting construction on Parker Dam without congressional authorization. Federal courts ultimately sided with California, but Arizona's resistance shaped the politics of water allocation for a generation.

Arizona v. California — 1963

The foundational legal case in Arizona water history. Arizona filed suit in the Supreme Court in 1952 against California, Nevada, and the United States, seeking clarification of each state's rights to Colorado River water. After eleven years of litigation — the longest case in the Supreme Court's history at that time — the Court issued its decision on June 3, 1963.

The Court ruled 5-3 for Arizona, holding that:

  • Arizona was entitled to 2.8 million acre-feet annually from the Lower Basin's 7.5 million share
  • California was entitled to 4.4 million acre-feet
  • Nevada was entitled to 300,000 acre-feet
  • The Secretary of the Interior had authority to allocate water during shortages
  • Native American tribes had federally reserved water rights that were senior to state water rights — a ruling with enormous long-term significance

The 1963 decision gave Arizona the legal right to 2.8 million acre-feet, but exercising that right required building a massive delivery system. That became the Central Arizona Project.

Central Arizona Project (CAP)

Authorization and Construction

Congress authorized the Central Arizona Project in 1968 as part of the Colorado River Basin Project Act. Construction began in 1973. The system was progressively completed and delivered its first water to central Arizona in 1985, reaching Tucson in 1992. Cost: over $4 billion in then-year dollars — the most expensive water project in U.S. history at the time of completion.

Technical Specifications

  • Total length: 336 miles from Lake Havasu to Tucson
  • Route: Lake Havasu → Granite Reef Aqueduct → Phoenix → Tucson
  • Lift: Water is pumped uphill 2,900 feet total elevation gain from river to Phoenix
  • 14 pumping plants along the system — the largest, Havasu Pumping Plant, lifts water 824 feet in a single stage
  • Capacity: 1.5 million acre-feet per year (at peak)
  • Energy consumption: The CAP is one of the largest electricity consumers in Arizona — 2,500+ MW of power to pump water annually, primarily supplied by the Navajo Generating Station (now retired) and Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station
  • Tunnel segments: 22 miles of lined tunnels through mountain ranges

Water Users

  • Municipal users (38%): Phoenix, Tucson, Mesa, Scottsdale, Chandler, Tempe, Gilbert, Peoria, Glendale, and dozens of smaller municipalities
  • Agriculture (34%): Pinal County farmers — historically the largest CAP water users, now facing severe cuts
  • Native American tribes (15%): Arizona's tribal nations hold significant CAP entitlements through water rights settlements
  • Groundwater recharge (13%): Excess CAP water is pumped into underground storage facilities (ASRs) for drought banking

The Tier System — Shortage Declarations

The Bureau of Reclamation operates the Colorado River under a shortage declaration system triggered by Lake Mead elevation levels. Once Lake Mead drops below certain thresholds, Tier levels activate, cutting deliveries to Lower Basin users. Arizona bears the most severe cuts due to its "junior" priority status under the Law of the River.

Tier 0 (Normal Operations)
Lake Mead above 1,075 feet elevation. Full allocations delivered. Arizona receives full 2.8M acre-feet CAP entitlement. No cuts.
Tier 1 — First Shortage
Lake Mead 1,050–1,075 ft. Arizona loses 512,000 acre-feet. Pinal County agriculture bears first cuts. Declared August 2021 — the first-ever shortage in Colorado River history.
Tier 2 / 2A
Lake Mead 1,025–1,050 ft. Arizona loses 592,000–640,000 acre-feet. Deeper agricultural cuts. Some municipal CAP deliveries reduced. Declared for 2022.
Tier 3 — Maximum Shortage
Lake Mead below 1,025 ft. Arizona loses 720,000 acre-feet — 25% of its total Colorado River entitlement. Severe agricultural cuts, municipal systems stressed. Declared for 2023.

Lake Mead & Lake Powell Status

The two largest reservoirs in the United States are the control points for Colorado River delivery:

  • Lake Mead (Nevada/Arizona border, Hoover Dam): Reached all-time record low of 1,040 feet in July 2022. Water recoveries from 2023 winter snowpack pushed it above 1,060 feet by mid-2023. Still well below pre-drought levels of 1,200+ feet. Full capacity: 1,221.4 feet.
  • Lake Powell (Utah/Arizona border, Glen Canyon Dam): Dropped to within 30 feet of "dead pool" — the level at which power generation and water delivery stop — in 2022. Snowpack recovery helped, but structural deficit remains.
  • Bureau of Reclamation 24-month study: Published twice yearly, the key official document projecting shortage tiers. Available at usbr.gov/lc/region/g4000/24mo.pdf

Tribal Water Rights — The Senior Priority

The 1963 Arizona v. California Supreme Court decision established that Native American tribes have federally reserved water rights dating to the establishment of their reservations — in many cases predating Arizona statehood (1912) and the Colorado River Compact (1922). These "Winters Doctrine" rights are senior to state water law.

Major Arizona Tribal Water Settlements

  • Gila River Indian Community (GRIC) — 2004 Settlement
    The largest water rights settlement in Arizona history. The Gila River IC (Pima and Maricopa peoples, 22,000 members, 372,000 acres) received 653,500 acre-feet per year — the largest single allocation in Arizona. Includes CAP water, Salt River Project deliveries, and groundwater. Value: approximately $1.65 billion in federal infrastructure and money.
  • Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community (SRPMIC) — 1988 Settlement
    122,000 acre-feet annually. Includes CAP water and Salt River Project entitlements. The SRPMIC's water holdings make them key participants in Phoenix area water negotiations.
  • Tohono O'odham Nation — 2010 Settlement
    The Tohono O'odham Nation covers 2.8 million acres in southwestern Arizona (the size of Connecticut), straddling the border with Mexico. Settlement included 66,000 acre-feet per year plus groundwater rights. The Nation's border position makes their water rights intersect with border security issues in complex ways.
  • Navajo Nation — UNRESOLVED
    The most significant unresolved tribal water rights claim in the Southwest. The Navajo Nation (covering northeastern Arizona, northwestern New Mexico, southeastern Utah) has never had a comprehensive water rights settlement. A 2023 Supreme Court case (Arizona v. Navajo Nation) ruled against the Navajo on federal trust duty to supply water, but their underlying rights claims remain. The Navajo Nation's potential claims could affect Upper Basin allocations significantly.

Arizona Groundwater Management Act — 1980

Facing a groundwater crisis decades before the current Colorado River shortage, Arizona passed the Groundwater Management Act (GMA) of 1980 — one of the most comprehensive groundwater laws in U.S. history. Key provisions:

  • Created Active Management Areas (AMAs) covering Phoenix, Tucson, Pinal County, Prescott, and Santa Cruz — the most populated and water-stressed regions
  • Required water providers in AMAs to demonstrate a 100-year assured water supply before approving new subdivisions
  • Established the Arizona Department of Water Resources (ADWR) to manage groundwater
  • Created a system of Groundwater Replenishment Districts for banking CAP water underground (Aquifer Storage and Recovery, ASR)

The 100-year assured supply requirement is now under stress. In 2023, ADWR announced that parts of the Phoenix AMA face groundwater shortfalls within the 100-year planning horizon — effectively invalidating the assured supply designations for some rapidly developing areas, including Rio Verde Highlands and parts of Queen Creek. Rio Verde Highlands, an unincorporated Scottsdale suburb, was cut off from City of Scottsdale water service in January 2023, leaving thousands of residents scrambling for water.

Agricultural Water — Pinal County at the Epicenter

Pinal County, between Phoenix and Tucson, has historically been one of the most water-intensive agricultural regions in the American Southwest. Cotton, alfalfa, and other crops grown on subsidized CAP water transformed the desert into farmland — but that transformation was built on water that doesn't exist during shortage conditions.

Under the Law of the River, Pinal County agricultural users hold the most junior water rights among CAP contractors. They are the first to be cut. The 2021-2023 shortage tiers eliminated Pinal County's CAP allocation almost entirely, forcing farmers to pump groundwater (which is running out), fallow fields, and sell water rights. The long-term trajectory is clear: Pinal County agriculture as practiced in the 20th century is not viable in the 21st.

Desalination — The Proposed Solution

Multiple proposals have emerged for desalination as a supplemental water source to reduce Colorado River dependence:

  • Gulf of California desalination plant (Puerto Peñasco, Sonora, Mexico) — Arizona, Nevada, and Mexico have jointly studied a large-scale seawater desalination plant on the Gulf of California, piping water to Arizona via a new pipeline. Estimated cost: $5-8 billion. Energy requirements: enormous. Status: feasibility studies ongoing; not funded.
  • Pacific Ocean desalination (California) — Proposed to reduce California's Colorado River dependence, freeing water for Arizona. California's Poseidon Water plant in Huntington Beach was rejected by state regulators in 2022 over environmental concerns, illustrating the political obstacles.
  • Brackish groundwater desalination — Treatment of slightly salty groundwater already in Arizona aquifers. Lower cost than seawater desalination, but limited volume.
